<h2 id="three-point">Three-point estimates</h2>

Asking "how long will this take?" gets you a single number that is usually optimistic, because
people estimate the path where nothing goes wrong. PERT asks for three instead:

- $a$ — **optimistic**: everything goes right. Roughly a 1-in-100 best case.
- $m$ — **most likely**: the modal outcome, what usually happens.
- $b$ — **pessimistic**: things go badly, though short of catastrophe. Roughly a 1-in-100 worst
  case.

Three questions produce a much better estimate than one, for a reason that has little to do with
mathematics: asking for a pessimistic case gives people **permission to think about what could go
wrong**, which a single-point question implicitly discourages.

<h2 id="expected-time">Expected time</h2>

The three estimates are combined as a weighted average:

$$
t_e = \frac{a + 4m + b}{6}
$$

The most likely value carries four-sixths of the weight and the extremes one-sixth each. This is
the mean of a beta distribution under particular shape assumptions — and note that $t_e$ is
generally **not** equal to $m$. When the pessimistic case is further from the mode than the
optimistic one, which is nearly always true for software work, the expected time exceeds the most
likely time.

That gap is the whole point. It is the arithmetic of the observation that projects overrun more
often than they underrun.

<h2 id="variance">Variance, and why it matters</h2>

$$
\sigma^2 = \left(\frac{b - a}{6}\right)^2
$$

The reasoning: for a roughly normal quantity almost all the mass lies within three standard
deviations of the mean, so the full range $b - a$ spans about six of them.

Variance is what makes PERT more than a smarter average. Two activities with the same expected
duration can carry very different risk, and only the variance distinguishes them — which is what
lesson&nbsp;13.4 needs to attach a probability to a delivery date.

estimates = pd.DataFrame([
    # code, name,                   a,   m,   b     (working days)
    ("A", "Requirements gathering",  4,   6,  10),
    ("B", "Data model design",       3,   5,   9),
    ("C", "API design",              2,   4,   8),
    ("D", "Backend services",        8,  12,  22),
    ("E", "Frontend application",    6,  10,  16),
    ("F", "Data migration",          5,   8,  15),
    ("G", "System integration",      3,   5,  11),
    ("H", "Testing",                 4,   7,  13),
    ("I", "Documentation",           2,   3,   6),
    ("J", "Launch",                  1,   2,   5),
], columns=["code", "name", "a", "m", "b"])

estimates["te"] = (estimates["a"] + 4 * estimates["m"] + estimates["b"]) / 6
estimates["variance"] = ((estimates["b"] - estimates["a"]) / 6) ** 2
estimates["sd"] = np.sqrt(estimates["variance"])
estimates["te - m"] = estimates["te"] - estimates["m"]

print(estimates.round(3).to_string(index=False))

print()
print(f"Every expected time exceeds its most likely value: "
      f"{bool((estimates['te - m'] > 0).all())}")
print(f"Total slippage from that effect alone: "
      f"{estimates['te - m'].sum():.2f} days across {len(estimates)} activities")

print()
print("Ranked by uncertainty rather than by duration:")
by_risk = estimates.sort_values("sd", ascending=False)
for _, row in by_risk.head(5).iterrows():
    print(f"  {row['code']}  {row['name']:<24} te {row['te']:>5.2f}  sd {row['sd']:>5.2f}"
          f"   range {row['a']:.0f}-{row['b']:.0f}")

print()
print("Backend services and data migration carry the most risk. They are")
print("where estimation effort and contingency planning should go, and that")
print("ranking is not the same as ranking by expected duration.")`}

<PythonCell label="What the beta distribution behind PERT looks like">
{`import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

def pert_beta(a, m, b, lam=4.0):
    """The beta distribution PERT implicitly assumes."""
    if b == a:
        return None
    mean = (a + lam * m + b) / (lam + 2)
    alpha = ((mean - a) * (2 * m - a - b)) / ((m - mean) * (b - a)) if m != mean else 3.0
    beta = alpha * (b - mean) / (mean - a)
    return stats.beta(alpha, beta, loc=a, scale=b - a)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(9.5, 3.2))
for ax, code in zip(axes, ["D", "E", "J"]):
    row = estimates[estimates["code"] == code].iloc[0]
    distribution = pert_beta(row["a"], row["m"], row["b"])
    x = np.linspace(row["a"], row["b"], 300)
    ax.plot(x, distribution.pdf(x), linewidth=2)
    ax.axvline(row["m"], linewidth=1.2, linestyle="--")
    ax.axvline(row["te"], linewidth=1.2)
    ax.set_title(f"{code}: {row['name']}", fontsize=8)
    ax.set_xlabel("days")
axes[0].set_ylabel("density")
plt.tight_layout()

print("Solid line = expected time, dashed = most likely.")
print("The distribution is right-skewed: things can go much more wrong")
print("than they can go right, which is why te sits above m.")

print()
print("Sanity-checking the formula against the fitted distributions:")
print(f"{'code':>5}{'te formula':>12}{'beta mean':>12}{'sd formula':>12}{'beta sd':>10}")
for _, row in estimates.iterrows():
    distribution = pert_beta(row["a"], row["m"], row["b"])
    print(f"{row['code']:>5}{row['te']:>12.3f}{distribution.mean():>12.3f}"
          f"{row['sd']:>12.3f}{distribution.std():>10.3f}")

print()
print("The means agree exactly, by construction. The standard deviations")
print("do not: (b-a)/6 is an approximation, and it generally understates")
print("the true spread. That matters in lesson 13.4.")`}
</PythonCell>

<Callout title="What PERT quietly assumes" variant="warning">
  <p>
    Three things, all debatable. That the range spans six standard deviations — for a genuinely
    skewed distribution it does not, and (b−a)/6 understates the spread. That the mode gets exactly
    four-sixths of the weight — a convention, not a derivation. And most importantly that your
    three estimates are honest: PERT cannot fix an optimistic <em>b</em>, and the pessimistic
    estimate is the one people most reliably under-state.
  </p>
</Callout>

Sometimes you face a decision where the outcomes depend on something you cannot predict and cannot
even assign probabilities to. A genuinely novel market, an unprecedented regulatory change, a
competitor's unannounced strategy.

You still have to decide. This lesson covers what can be said in that situation — and what
cannot.

<h2 id="payoff-tables">Payoff tables</h2>

Every decision problem of this kind has the same structure:

- **Alternatives** — the courses of action available to you. Rows.
- **States of nature** — the things that might happen, outside your control. Columns.
- **Payoffs** — the outcome for each combination. Cells.

The scenario: a manufacturer is deciding how much to expand capacity ahead of an uncertain market.
Payoffs are the net present value in thousands of pounds.

| | Low demand | Moderate demand | High demand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Do nothing** | 10 | −5 | −30 |
| **Small expansion** | −20 | 60 | 80 |
| **Medium expansion** | −60 | 40 | 140 |
| **Large expansion** | −120 | 10 | 220 |

Doing nothing is cheapest if demand stays low but forfeits the upside and cedes market share.
Large expansion is transformative if demand is high and ruinous if it is not.

<Callout title="States of nature must be exhaustive and mutually exclusive">
  Exactly one state will occur. If two of your columns could both happen, or if some possible
  future fits none of them, the table is malformed and every criterion computed from it will be
  wrong. This is the same discipline as defining outcomes in chapter&nbsp;2.
</Callout>

<h2 id="maximax">Maximax: the optimist</h2>

Find the best possible payoff for each alternative; choose the alternative whose best is best.

$$
\text{choose } \arg\max_i \left(\max_j\, V_{ij}\right)
$$

This criterion looks only at the best case and ignores everything else. It is how you decide if
you believe things will go well — or if you have so little downside exposure that the worst case
does not matter.

<h2 id="maximin">Maximin: the pessimist</h2>

Find the worst possible payoff for each alternative; choose the alternative whose worst is best.

$$
\text{choose } \arg\max_i \left(\min_j\, V_{ij}\right)
$$

Also called the Wald criterion. It optimises the guaranteed floor and ignores the upside entirely.
This is the right criterion when the worst case is genuinely unsurvivable — bankruptcy, safety
failure, loss of licence — because in those situations the expected value is irrelevant if you do
not live to collect it.

<h2 id="minimax-regret">Minimax regret</h2>

The third criterion asks a different question: not *how good is the outcome*, but *how much worse
is it than what I could have achieved had I known?*

**Regret** is the difference between a cell and the best payoff available in its column:

$$
R_{ij} = \left(\max_k V_{kj}\right) - V_{ij}
$$

Then minimise the maximum regret across states:

$$
\text{choose } \arg\min_i \left(\max_j R_{ij}\right)
$$

This is the Savage criterion, and it captures something real — the feeling of having chosen badly
in hindsight — while being less extreme than either maximax or maximin.

<PythonCell label="All three criteria on the same table">
{`import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

alternatives = ["do nothing", "small expansion", "medium expansion", "large expansion"]
states = ["low demand", "moderate demand", "high demand"]

payoffs = np.array([
    [  10,  -5,  -30],
    [ -20,  60,   80],
    [ -60,  40,  140],
    [-120,  10,  220],
], dtype=float)

table = pd.DataFrame(payoffs, index=alternatives, columns=states)
print("Payoff table (NPV, GBP thousands):")
print(table.to_string())

best_case = payoffs.max(axis=1)
worst_case = payoffs.min(axis=1)

print()
print(f"{'alternative':<20}{'best case':>12}{'worst case':>13}")
for name, best, worst in zip(alternatives, best_case, worst_case):
    print(f"{name:<20}{best:>12,.0f}{worst:>13,.0f}")

print()
print(f"MAXIMAX  -> {alternatives[best_case.argmax()]}  "
      f"(best of the best cases: {best_case.max():,.0f})")
print(f"MAXIMIN  -> {alternatives[worst_case.argmax()]}  "
      f"(best of the worst cases: {worst_case.max():,.0f})")`}
</PythonCell>

<PythonCell label="The regret table">
{`column_best = payoffs.max(axis=0)
regret = column_best - payoffs

regret_table = pd.DataFrame(regret, index=alternatives, columns=states)
print("Best achievable payoff in each state:")
for state, value in zip(states, column_best):
    print(f"  {state:<18}{value:>8,.0f}")

print()
print("Regret table (how much worse than the best you could have done):")
print(regret_table.to_string())

max_regret = regret.max(axis=1)
print()
print(f"{'alternative':<20}{'max regret':>12}")
for name, value in zip(alternatives, max_regret):
    print(f"{name:<20}{value:>12,.0f}")

print()
print(f"MINIMAX REGRET -> {alternatives[max_regret.argmin()]}  "
      f"(smallest maximum regret: {max_regret.min():,.0f})")

print()
print("=" * 56)
print("THE THREE CRITERIA DISAGREE:")
print(f"  maximax        -> {alternatives[best_case.argmax()]}")
print(f"  maximin        -> {alternatives[worst_case.argmax()]}")
print(f"  minimax regret -> {alternatives[max_regret.argmin()]}")
print("=" * 56)`}
</PythonCell>

Three defensible criteria, three different recommendations, one payoff table. This is not a defect
in the methods — it is the honest content of a decision made without probabilities.

What the analysis has done is genuinely useful even so. It has ruled out nothing arbitrarily,
exposed exactly what each recommendation assumes about your attitude to risk, and made that
attitude an explicit choice rather than an unexamined one. **The criterion is a statement about
your risk preferences, and it should be chosen before you see which alternative it favours.**

<Callout title="Choose the criterion first" variant="warning">
  Computing all three and then picking the one whose answer you like is not decision analysis —
  it is rationalising a decision you had already made. Decide in advance whether you are
  protecting a floor, chasing an upside, or minimising hindsight regret, and commit to what that
  choice implies.
</Callout>

<PythonCell label="A fourth option: weighting optimism explicitly">
{`# The Hurwicz criterion blends best and worst case with a coefficient of
# optimism alpha, so maximax (alpha=1) and maximin (alpha=0) are its endpoints.
print(f"{'alpha':>7}  " + "".join(f"{name[:12]:>14}" for name in alternatives))
for alpha in [0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0]:
    values = alpha * best_case + (1 - alpha) * worst_case
    marks = "".join(
        f"{value:>13,.1f}" + ("*" if index == values.argmax() else " ")
        for index, value in enumerate(values)
    )
    print(f"{alpha:>7.2f}  {marks}")

print()
print("* marks the preferred alternative at each level of optimism.")
print()
print("Switching points - the alpha at which the choice changes:")
previous = None
for alpha in np.linspace(0, 1, 1001):
    choice = alternatives[(alpha * best_case + (1 - alpha) * worst_case).argmax()]
    if choice != previous:
        print(f"  from alpha = {alpha:.3f}: {choice}")
        previous = choice

print()
print("Making the switching points explicit is more useful than any single")
print("recommendation: it tells you how optimistic you would have to be")
print("before your answer changes.")`}
</PythonCell>

The dataset for this chapter is 1,800 consumer loans with credit score, income, loan amount,
debt-to-income ratio, employment history, prior defaults, and whether the loan ultimately
defaulted. It is synthetic, generated from a known log-odds model, so once again we can compare
what the fit recovers against what is actually there.

<h2 id="the-data">The data</h2>

<PythonCell label="Exploring before fitting">
{`import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

loans = pd.read_csv("loan-defaults.csv")

print(loans.shape)
print(loans.head().to_string(index=False))
print()
print(f"default rate: {loans['defaulted'].mean():.4f}  "
      f"({loans['defaulted'].sum()} of {len(loans)})")

print()
print("How each predictor differs between the two groups:")
numeric = ["credit_score", "annual_income", "loan_amount",
           "debt_to_income", "months_employed", "prior_defaults"]
comparison = loans.groupby("defaulted")[numeric].mean().T
comparison.columns = ["repaid", "defaulted"]
comparison["difference"] = comparison["defaulted"] - comparison["repaid"]
print(comparison.round(3).to_string())`}
</PythonCell>

The direction of every difference is intuitive: defaulters have lower credit scores, higher
debt-to-income ratios, shorter employment histories and more prior defaults. That is reassuring,
but a table of group means cannot tell you which of those matter *net of the others* — several of
them move together. That is what the regression is for.

<Callout title="A 21% base rate is worth noting now">
  <p>
    Roughly one loan in five defaults. Keep that number in mind: lesson&nbsp;5.3 shows that a model
    predicting "no default" for every single loan would score 79% accuracy, which sounds
    respectable and is completely useless.
  </p>
</Callout>

<h2 id="fitting">Fitting the model</h2>

The formula interface is identical to `ols`; only the function name changes. Under the hood the
difference is larger: there is no closed-form solution, so the coefficients are found by
**maximum likelihood** — iteratively searching for the parameters that make the observed
outcomes most probable.

<PythonCell label="Fitting a multiple logistic regression">
{`import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

model = smf.logit(
    "defaulted ~ credit_score + debt_to_income + months_employed + prior_defaults",
    data=loans,
).fit()

print(model.summary())`}
</PythonCell>

The summary is close to the linear one, with three differences worth naming.

**`z` instead of `t`.** Maximum-likelihood estimates are asymptotically normal, so the test
statistic is a z rather than a t. Interpretation is unchanged.

**`Pseudo R-squ.` instead of `R-squared`.** There is no variance to decompose here, so this is
McFadden's pseudo-R², $1 - \ln L / \ln L_0$: how much better the fitted model's log-likelihood is
than an intercept-only model's. It is **not** on the same scale as an ordinary R². Values of 0.2
to 0.4 indicate a good fit; anything near 0.7 usually means something has leaked into the
predictors.

**`LLR p-value`.** The likelihood-ratio test, playing the role the F-test played in
chapter&nbsp;4 — does the model beat an intercept alone?

<h2 id="odds-ratios">Coefficients as odds ratios</h2>

Raw logistic coefficients are changes in log-odds, which nobody has intuition for. Exponentiating
turns them into odds ratios, which are directly quotable.

$$
\text{odds ratio} = e^{\beta_j}
$$

An odds ratio of 1 means no effect. Above 1 means the odds increase; below 1 means they decrease.
And because the effect is multiplicative, a change of $c$ units multiplies the odds by
$e^{\beta_j c}$ — which is how you rescale to units people care about.

<PythonCell label="Odds ratios, with intervals and in useful units">
{`odds_ratios = pd.DataFrame({
    "coefficient": model.params,
    "odds_ratio": np.exp(model.params),
    "or_ci_low": np.exp(model.conf_int()[0]),
    "or_ci_high": np.exp(model.conf_int()[1]),
    "p_value": model.pvalues,
})
print(odds_ratios.round(4).to_string())

print()
print("Rescaled to differences that actually occur in the data:")
per_unit = model.params

print(f"  100 points of credit score : odds x {np.exp(per_unit['credit_score'] * 100):.4f}")
print(f"  0.10 more debt-to-income   : odds x {np.exp(per_unit['debt_to_income'] * 0.10):.4f}")
print(f"  2 more years employed      : odds x {np.exp(per_unit['months_employed'] * 24):.4f}")
print(f"  1 prior default            : odds x {np.exp(per_unit['prior_defaults'] * 1):.4f}")

print()
print("Stated in plain language:")
print(f"  A borrower with 100 more points of credit score has "
      f"{(1 - np.exp(per_unit['credit_score'] * 100)) * 100:.0f}% lower odds of default,")
print("  holding debt-to-income, employment and prior defaults fixed.")`}
</PythonCell>

<Callout title="Odds ratios are not risk ratios" variant="pitfall">
  <p>
    "The odds double" and "the probability doubles" are different claims, and they only coincide
    when the probability is small. At a base probability of 0.05 an odds ratio of 2 raises the
    probability to about 0.095 — nearly double. At a base of 0.5 the same odds ratio raises it only
    to 0.667. Reporting an odds ratio as though it were a relative risk overstates the effect
    whenever the outcome is common, and default at 21% is common enough to matter.
  </p>
</Callout>

<PythonCell label="Recovering the generating model, and predicting">
{`print("The data were generated with log-odds:")
print("  -0.9 - 0.011*(credit_score - 650) + 2.2*debt_to_income")
print("       - 0.020*min(months_employed, 120) + 0.62*prior_defaults")
print()
truth = {"credit_score": -0.011, "debt_to_income": 2.2,
         "months_employed": -0.020, "prior_defaults": 0.62}
ci = model.conf_int()
print(f"{'term':<18}{'estimate':>11}{'truth':>9}   95% CI")
for name, value in truth.items():
    lo, hi = ci.loc[name]
    print(f"{name:<18}{model.params[name]:>11.5f}{value:>9.4f}   [{lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f}]")

print()
print("Predicted probability for three example borrowers:")
applicants = pd.DataFrame({
    "credit_score":    [780, 690, 560],
    "debt_to_income":  [0.18, 0.34, 0.58],
    "months_employed": [96, 40, 6],
    "prior_defaults":  [0, 0, 1],
}, index=["strong", "typical", "weak"])

applicants["P(default)"] = model.predict(applicants)
applicants["odds"] = applicants["P(default)"] / (1 - applicants["P(default)"])
print(applicants.round(4).to_string())`}
</PythonCell>

Every true coefficient falls inside its confidence interval, and the predicted probabilities span
a wide range — from a few percent for a strong applicant to well over half for a weak one. The
model has genuinely learned something.

Whether it is *useful* is a separate question, and it is the one lesson&nbsp;5.3 takes up.

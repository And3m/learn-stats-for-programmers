Variable data is measured on a continuous scale — a length, a weight, a temperature. It carries far
more information per observation than attribute data, so variable charts detect shifts with much
smaller samples.

They always come in **pairs**: one chart for the process centre and one for its spread. A process
can drift in either without the other moving, and a single chart would miss half of what matters.

<h2 id="xbar-and-r">x-bar and R</h2>

The classical pairing. For each subgroup, compute the mean $\bar{x}$ and the range
$R = \max - \min$.

$$
\bar{\bar{x}} = \text{mean of subgroup means}, \qquad \bar{R} = \text{mean of subgroup ranges}
$$

$$
\text{x-bar chart:} \quad \bar{\bar{x}} \pm A_2\bar{R}
\qquad
\text{R chart:} \quad UCL = D_4\bar{R},\; LCL = D_3\bar{R}
$$

The constants $A_2$, $D_3$ and $D_4$ depend only on the subgroup size and are tabulated. They
exist because $\bar{R}/d_2$ is an unbiased estimator of $\sigma$, and the constants fold that
conversion together with the factor of 3.

<Callout title="Read the R chart first" variant="warning">
  The x-bar limits are computed <em>from</em> R̄. If the spread is out of control, R̄ is not a
  meaningful estimate of anything, and the x-bar limits built on it are wrong. Always establish
  that the spread is stable before interpreting the centre — this is the single most-violated
  rule in control charting.
</Callout>

<h2 id="xbar-and-s">x-bar and s</h2>

The range uses only two of the observations in each subgroup — the largest and smallest — and
throws the rest away. The standard deviation uses all of them, so it is a more efficient estimator
of spread.

$$
\bar{s} = \text{mean of subgroup standard deviations}
$$

$$
\text{x-bar chart:} \quad \bar{\bar{x}} \pm A_3\bar{s}
\qquad
\text{s chart:} \quad UCL = B_4\bar{s},\; LCL = B_3\bar{s}
$$

The range survives in practice mainly because it can be computed by hand on the shop floor. With
a computer there is little reason not to use $s$, and for subgroups larger than about 10 the range
becomes markedly inefficient.

<h2 id="subgroup-size">Subgroup size</h2>

Subgroup size controls how quickly a shift is detected, through the standard error of the mean:

$$
\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

Larger subgroups give tighter x-bar limits and faster detection, at proportionally more inspection
cost. The conventional guidance:

- $n = 4$ or $5$ — the standard choice; use the range.
- $n \ge 10$ — use $s$ rather than $R$; the range is losing too much information.
- $n = 1$ — no subgroup is possible; use the I-MR chart in the next lesson.

The **average run length** — how many subgroups pass, on average, before a shift is flagged — is
the honest way to compare designs.

<PythonCell label="Average run length by subgroup size">
{`from scipy import stats

sigma = rbar / k["d2"]

def average_run_length(shift_in_sigmas, n):
    """Expected subgroups until an x-bar chart signals, for a given shift."""
    shift = shift_in_sigmas * np.sqrt(n)          # in units of the mean's SE
    beta = stats.norm.cdf(3 - shift) - stats.norm.cdf(-3 - shift)
    return 1 / (1 - beta)

print(f"process sigma estimated at {sigma:.4f}")
print()
print(f"{'shift':>8}" + "".join(f"{f'n={n}':>10}" for n in [1, 2, 4, 5, 9, 16]))
for shift in [0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0]:
    row = "".join(f"{average_run_length(shift, n):>10.1f}" for n in [1, 2, 4, 5, 9, 16])
    label = "none" if shift == 0 else f"{shift:.1f} sd"
    print(f"{label:>8}{row}")

print()
print("The 'no shift' row is the false-alarm run length: about 370")
print("subgroups regardless of n, which is what three-sigma limits buy.")
print()
print("A 1-sigma shift takes ~44 subgroups to catch with n=1, but only")
print("~5 with n=5 and ~2 with n=16. That is the value of subgrouping.")

fig, ax = plt.subplots()

<h2 id="does-order-matter">Does order matter?</h2>

Almost every counting mistake in elementary probability comes from getting one question wrong:
**does order matter?**

- A four-digit PIN. `1234` and `4321` are different PINs. Order matters.
- A five-card poker hand. The same five cards dealt in any sequence is the same hand. Order does
  not matter.
- The first three finishers in a race. Gold, silver and bronze are distinct positions. Order
  matters.
- A three-person committee drawn from a team. Being on the committee is being on the committee.
  Order does not matter.

Get this wrong and your answer is off by a factor of $k!$ — which for a five-card hand is 120, so
it is not a subtle error.

<h2 id="permutations">Permutations</h2>

A permutation is an **ordered** arrangement. The number of ways to arrange $k$ items chosen from
$n$ distinct items, without repetition, is

$$
P(n, k) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}
$$

The reasoning is the multiplication rule: $n$ choices for the first slot, $n-1$ for the second
(one item is used up), down to $n-k+1$ for the last. Writing that product as a ratio of
factorials is just notational convenience.

Three medals among eight runners: $P(8,3) = \frac{8!}{5!} = 8 \times 7 \times 6 = 336$ possible
podiums.

When $k = n$ the formula gives $n!$ — the number of ways to arrange everything. Eight runners can
finish in $8! = 40{,}320$ different orders.

<Callout title="Repetition changes the formula" variant="warning">
  <p>
    The formula above assumes items are used up as they are chosen. If repetition is allowed — as
    with a PIN, where digits can repeat — the count is simply <em>n<sup>k</sup></em>. A four-digit
    PIN has 10<sup>4</sup> = 10,000 possibilities, not P(10,4) = 5,040. Read the problem carefully
    for whether items are replaced.
  </p>
</Callout>

<h2 id="combinations">Combinations</h2>

A combination is an **unordered** selection. Since every unordered group of $k$ items can be
arranged in $k!$ different orders, and permutations count each of those separately, you get
combinations by dividing the permutation count by $k!$:

$$
C(n, k) = \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} = \frac{P(n,k)}{k!}
$$

Three people from a team of eight: $\binom{8}{3} = \frac{336}{6} = 56$ possible committees, versus
336 possible podiums from the same eight people. The 6 is $3!$ — the number of orderings of each
group of three that a podium distinguishes and a committee does not.

Two properties are worth internalising:

$$
\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} = 1
$$

The first says choosing which 3 people join is the same as choosing which 5 stay out. The second
is the convention that there is exactly one way to choose nothing, and one way to choose
everything — which matters when these terms appear inside a sum, as they do in the binomial
distribution in chapter&nbsp;3.

<h2 id="in-python">Counting in Python</h2>

Both functions live in the standard library's `math` module; SciPy's versions accept arrays,
which is occasionally useful.

<PythonCell label="Permutations and combinations">
{`import math
from scipy.special import comb, perm

n, k = 8, 3

print(f"permutations P({n},{k}) = {math.perm(n, k):,}   (order matters)")
print(f"combinations C({n},{k}) = {math.comb(n, k):,}   (order does not)")
print(f"ratio                   = {math.perm(n, k) // math.comb(n, k)} = {k}! = {math.factorial(k)}")

print()
print("A five-card poker hand from 52 cards:")
hands = math.comb(52, 5)
print(f"  C(52,5) = {hands:,} distinct hands")
print(f"  P(52,5) = {math.perm(52, 5):,} ordered deals — {math.factorial(5)}x more")

print()
print("SciPy's vectorised versions agree:")
print(" ", comb(8, [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]).astype(int), "<- C(8,k) for k = 0..4")
print(" ", perm(8, [0, 1, 2, 3]).astype(int), "<- P(8,k) for k = 0..3")`}
</PythonCell>

Now put combinations to work on an actual probability question. The probability of being dealt a
flush — five cards all of the same suit — is the number of flush hands divided by the number of
hands. There are 4 suits, and $\binom{13}{5}$ ways to pick five cards within a suit.

<PythonCell label="The probability of a flush">
{`import math

total_hands = math.comb(52, 5)
flush_hands = 4 * math.comb(13, 5)          # 4 suits, C(13,5) hands within each

# Straight flushes are usually counted separately, so subtract them:
straight_flushes = 4 * 10                    # 10 starting ranks per suit (A-5 through 10-A)
plain_flushes = flush_hands - straight_flushes

print(f"all hands       {total_hands:>10,}")
print(f"flush hands     {flush_hands:>10,}")
print(f"  of which straight flushes {straight_flushes}")
print(f"plain flushes   {plain_flushes:>10,}")
print()
print(f"P(flush, incl. straight flush) = {flush_hands / total_hands:.6f}  (1 in {total_hands / flush_hands:.0f})")
print(f"P(plain flush)                 = {plain_flushes / total_hands:.6f}  (1 in {total_hands / plain_flushes:.0f})")`}
</PythonCell>

<Callout title="Why this matters later">
  <p>
    Combinations are not a party trick. The binomial coefficient is the first term of the binomial
    distribution in chapter&nbsp;3 — it counts <em>how many ways</em> k successes can be arranged
    among n trials, which is exactly the "does order matter" question applied to a sequence of
    coin flips. If you are comfortable here, that formula will look obvious rather than arbitrary.
  </p>
</Callout>

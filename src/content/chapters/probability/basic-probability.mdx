Probability is the language every later chapter is written in. Regression coefficients come with
p-values; classifiers emit probabilities; control charts are built from tail areas; Monte Carlo
simulation is nothing but sampling from distributions. It is worth being unusually careful here,
because errors made at this level propagate everywhere.

<h2 id="defining-probability">Successes over outcomes</h2>

For an experiment whose outcomes are all equally likely, the probability of an event $A$ is

$$
P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in which } A \text{ occurs}}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}}
$$

Flip a fair coin and call heads: one favourable outcome out of two, so $P(\text{heads}) = 1/2$.
Draw one card from a standard deck and ask for a face card: there are twelve face cards — jack,
queen and king in each of four suits — out of fifty-two, so

$$
P(\text{face card}) = \frac{12}{52} = \frac{3}{13} \approx 0.2308
$$

Every probability obeys two rules that follow immediately from the definition: $0 \le P(A) \le 1$,
and the probabilities of all distinct outcomes of an experiment sum to exactly 1.

<Callout title="Two assumptions hiding in that fraction" variant="warning">
  The formula is only valid when <strong>every outcome is equally likely</strong>. That holds
  for fair coins and fair dice. It emphatically does not hold for "it either rains tomorrow or
  it does not, so the probability of rain is one half" — a genuinely common error. The second
  assumption is that everyone agrees what counts as a success, which is less trivial than it
  sounds when the event is something like "the deployment was a failure".
</Callout>

<h2 id="the-denominator">Getting the denominator right</h2>

The numerator is usually easy. The denominator is where people go wrong, and it goes wrong in a
predictable way: by counting *distinguishable results* instead of *equally likely outcomes*.

Roll two six-sided dice and ask for the probability of double sixes. It is tempting to reason
that the sum can be anything from 2 to 12 — eleven possibilities — and that double sixes is one
of them, giving $1/11$. That is wrong, because those eleven sums are not equally likely. There is
exactly one way to roll a 2 and six ways to roll a 7.

Count the underlying outcomes instead. Each die independently shows one of six faces, so there
are $6 \times 6 = 36$ equally likely outcomes, and exactly one of them is double sixes:

$$
P(\text{double six}) = \frac{1}{36} \approx 0.0278
$$

The same care is needed when drawing without replacement. Draw two cards from a deck: the first
draw has 52 possibilities, the second has only 51, so there are $52 \times 51 = 2652$ ordered
outcomes. Forgetting that the deck shrank is the most common slip in the whole subject.

Independence is the other place intuition misfires. If you have flipped a fair coin and seen
heads, the probability of heads on the next flip is still $1/2$. If thirty-five rolls of two dice
have produced no double six, the probability on the thirty-sixth is still $1/36$. Nothing about
the coin or the dice has changed. The law of large numbers says the *proportion* of heads
converges to one half over many flips; it says nothing whatsoever about the next flip, and
believing otherwise is the gambler's fallacy.

<PythonCell label="Empirical probability converges — the next flip does not care">
{`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 36 possible outcomes when rolling two dice; simulate a lot of them.
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(200_000, 2))
double_six = np.all(rolls == 6, axis=1)

print(f"simulated P(double six) = {double_six.mean():.5f}")
print(f"theoretical             = {1/36:.5f}")

# The gambler's fallacy, tested directly: after a run of non-double-sixes,
# is the next roll any more likely to be a double six?
after_drought = double_six[1:][~double_six[:-1]]
print()
print(f"P(double six | previous roll was not) = {after_drought.mean():.5f}")`}
</PythonCell>

<h2 id="three-types">Theoretical, empirical and subjective</h2>

The dice calculations above are **theoretical** probabilities: derived from an assumed model of
equally likely outcomes, without collecting any data. They are exact, conditional on the model
being right.

**Empirical** probability comes from observation instead:

$$
P(A) = \frac{\text{number of times } A \text{ was observed}}{\text{number of observations}}
$$

If it rained on 75 of the last 365 days, the empirical probability of rain on an arbitrary day is
$75/365 \approx 0.205$. This requires no model of *why* it rains — but it inherits every bias in
how the observations were collected, and it is only as stable as the process that generated them.
The simulation cell above computed an empirical probability and compared it to a theoretical one;
they agreed to three decimal places, which is exactly what the law of large numbers predicts.

**Subjective** probability is a considered degree of belief: a forecaster saying there is a 40%
chance a project slips, an engineer putting 10% on a particular root cause. It is not arbitrary —
a well-calibrated expert's subjective probabilities are genuinely informative — but it varies
between people and cannot be checked against a single outcome. Chapter&nbsp;13 uses subjective
three-point estimates as the raw material for project scheduling.

<h2 id="odds">Odds, and why gamblers prefer them</h2>

Odds express the same information as a ratio of favourable to unfavourable outcomes rather than
favourable to total. If $P$ is a probability, the odds in favour are

$$
\text{odds} = \frac{P}{1 - P}
\qquad\text{and conversely}\qquad
P = \frac{\text{odds}}{1 + \text{odds}}
$$

A probability of $0.75$ is odds of $3$, usually written 3:1 in favour. A probability of $0.2$ is
odds of $0.25$, or 1:4.

This is not merely a betting convention. Odds are what logistic regression actually models — its
coefficients are changes in *log*-odds, and exponentiating one gives an odds ratio. Chapter&nbsp;5
depends entirely on being comfortable moving between the two scales, so it is worth getting the
conversion into your fingers now.

<PythonCell label="Probability and odds, both directions">
{`import numpy as np

def to_odds(p):
    return p / (1 - p)

def to_probability(odds):
    return odds / (1 + odds)

for p in [0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 0.99]:
    odds = to_odds(p)
    print(f"P = {p:5.2f}   odds = {odds:8.3f}   log-odds = {np.log(odds):7.3f}")

# The round trip is exact.
print()
print("recovered:", [round(to_probability(to_odds(p)), 10) for p in [0.05, 0.2, 0.75]])`}
</PythonCell>

Notice the log-odds column. It is symmetric around zero — a probability of $0.5$ gives log-odds
of exactly $0$, and $p$ and $1-p$ give log-odds equal in magnitude and opposite in sign. That
symmetry, plus the fact that log-odds range over the whole real line while probabilities are
trapped in $[0, 1]$, is precisely why logistic regression works on that scale.

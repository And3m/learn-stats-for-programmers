Every model so far assumed observations were independent. Time series break that assumption
completely — today's value is related to yesterday's, and that dependence is not a nuisance to be
corrected. It is the signal.

<h2 id="the-distinction">The distinction</h2>

"Forecast" and "prediction" get used interchangeably; keeping them apart is useful.

A **prediction** maps features to an outcome. Given engine size, weight and power, predict fuel
economy. Order does not matter; you could shuffle the rows and lose nothing.

A **forecast** extends a sequence forward in time. Given sessions every day for four years,
estimate the next four weeks. Order is everything, and the only feature you have is the past of
the series itself.

Two consequences follow immediately, and both are places people go wrong.

**You cannot shuffle before splitting.** A random train/test split lets the model learn from
Wednesday to predict Tuesday, which is information it will never have in production. Time-series
validation must split chronologically — train on the past, test on the future.

**Uncertainty compounds with horizon.** A one-step forecast is usually decent. A fifty-step
forecast inherits every earlier error. Forecast intervals widen with the horizon, and if yours do
not, something is wrong.

<h2 id="components">Trend, seasonality, noise</h2>

The classical decomposition treats a series as three parts:

$$
y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t \quad\text{(additive)}
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t \quad\text{(multiplicative)}
$$

- **Trend** ($T_t$) — the long-run direction, after the wobble is smoothed away.
- **Seasonality** ($S_t$) — a pattern that repeats on a fixed known period. Day of week, month of
  year, hour of day.
- **Remainder** ($R_t$) — what is left. If the decomposition is good, this looks like noise.

Choose additive when the seasonal swing is roughly the same size regardless of level; choose
multiplicative when the swing grows with the level. A quick test: plot the series and ask whether
the peaks and troughs diverge over time.

<Callout title="Seasonality has a fixed period. Cycles do not." variant="warning">
  Seasonality repeats on a known, fixed schedule — every 7 days, every 12 months. A{" "}
  <em>cycle</em> — a business cycle, a boom and bust — has no fixed length and cannot be handled
  the same way. Decomposition models seasonality. Cycles end up in the trend or the remainder,
  which is one reason long-horizon economic forecasting is so hard.
</Callout>

<h2 id="the-benchmark">Establish a benchmark first</h2>

Before fitting anything, know what trivial methods achieve. Two are standard:

- **Naive**: tomorrow equals today. $\hat{y}_{t+h} = y_t$.
- **Seasonal naive**: tomorrow equals the same weekday last week. $\hat{y}_{t+h} = y_{t+h-m}$.

A model that cannot beat these has earned nothing, and the second is much harder to beat than
people expect on strongly seasonal data. Skipping this step is how sophisticated-looking
forecasting projects end up worse than a one-line rule.

<PythonCell label="What the trivial methods achieve">
{`horizon = 28
train, test = sessions[:-horizon], sessions[-horizon:]

def mape(actual, forecast):
    return float(np.mean(np.abs((np.asarray(actual) - np.asarray(forecast)) / np.asarray(actual))) * 100)

def mae(actual, forecast):
    return float(np.mean(np.abs(np.asarray(actual) - np.asarray(forecast))))

naive = np.full(horizon, train.iloc[-1])
seasonal_naive = np.array([train.iloc[-7 + (i % 7)] for i in range(horizon)])
mean_forecast = np.full(horizon, train.mean())

print(f"Forecasting {horizon} days ahead:")
print(f"{'method':<20}{'MAPE %':>9}{'MAE':>10}")
for name, forecast in [
    ("mean of history", mean_forecast),
    ("naive (last value)", naive),
    ("seasonal naive", seasonal_naive),
]:
    print(f"{name:<20}{mape(test, forecast):>9.2f}{mae(test, forecast):>10,.0f}")

print()
print("Seasonal naive is the number to beat. Any model that cannot")
print("improve on 'same weekday last week' is not earning its complexity.")

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
context = sessions[-(horizon + 56):]
ax.plot(context.index, context.values, linewidth=1, label="actual")
ax.plot(test.index, seasonal_naive, linewidth=2, linestyle="--", label="seasonal naive")

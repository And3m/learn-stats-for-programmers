Exponential smoothing comes at forecasting from a different direction than ARIMA. Instead of
modelling the correlation structure, it takes a weighted average of the past where the weights
decay exponentially — recent observations count more, and older ones fade smoothly rather than
being cut off.

The family is simpler, much faster to fit, and frequently just as accurate.

<h2 id="ses">Simple exponential smoothing</h2>

The level is updated each period as a blend of the new observation and the previous level:

$$
\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1 - \alpha)\ell_{t-1}, \qquad 0 < \alpha < 1
$$

Unrolling that recursion shows where the name comes from:

$$
\ell_t = \alpha y_t + \alpha(1-\alpha) y_{t-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)^2 y_{t-2} + \cdots
$$

Every past observation contributes, with geometrically decaying weight. $\alpha$ near 1 means the
forecast tracks the data closely and reacts fast; $\alpha$ near 0 means it is smooth and slow. It
is a responsiveness-versus-stability dial, and it is fitted from the data rather than chosen by
hand.

The critical limitation: SES has **no trend and no seasonality**, so its forecast is a flat line
at the current level. Useful for a stable series, useless for this one.

<h2 id="holt">Adding a trend</h2>

**Holt's linear method** adds a second smoothed component for the slope:

$$
\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})
$$
$$
b_t = \beta(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta) b_{t-1}
$$

and forecasts $h$ steps ahead as $\ell_t + h\,b_t$ — a straight line from the current level at the
current slope.

That linear extrapolation is dangerous at long horizons: a slope estimated from recent data,
extended twenty periods out, produces confident nonsense. **Damped trend** fixes it by shrinking
the slope with each step ahead using a damping parameter $\phi < 1$, so the forecast flattens out.
It is very often the better default.

<h2 id="holt-winters">Holt-Winters and seasonality</h2>

Add a third smoothed component for the seasonal pattern and you have **Holt-Winters**, with three
equations and three parameters ($\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$) plus the seasonal period $m$.

Seasonality can be additive or multiplicative, on the same criterion as in lesson&nbsp;7.1: does
the seasonal swing stay a constant size, or does it grow with the level?

<h2 id="choosing">Choosing a model</h2>

| Prefer | When |
| --- | --- |
| **Exponential smoothing** | You need something fast and robust; the series has clear trend and seasonality; you have limited history; you want few parameters to explain |
| **ARIMA** | The autocorrelation structure is genuinely complex; you need explicit modelling of that structure; you want to include external regressors |
| **Neither** | A naive or seasonal-naive rule already performs comparably — as it does here |

Two practical notes. Exponential smoothing fits in a fraction of a second where seasonal ARIMA
takes seconds, which matters when you are forecasting thousands of series. And the two families
overlap mathematically: every exponential smoothing model has an equivalent state-space form, and
several correspond exactly to particular ARIMA models. They are less rival philosophies than
different parameterisations of similar ideas.

<Callout title="A default worth adopting">
  <p>
    Start with seasonal naive. Then try damped Holt-Winters. Only reach for ARIMA if neither is
    good enough and you have evidence the residual autocorrelation structure is worth modelling.
    This ordering costs minutes and prevents a great deal of wasted effort.
  </p>
</Callout>

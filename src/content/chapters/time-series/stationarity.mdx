<h2 id="what-it-means">What stationarity means</h2>

A series is **weakly stationary** if three things hold at every point in time:

1. Constant mean — no trend.
2. Constant variance — the wobble does not grow or shrink.
3. Autocovariance depending only on the lag, not on when you look. The relationship between today
   and a week ago is the same in 2021 as in 2024.

Why insist on this? Because ARIMA and its relatives estimate **one** set of parameters and apply
them everywhere. If the mean drifts, a single mean is a fiction. If the variance grows, one
variance is a fiction. The model is not merely inaccurate on non-stationary data — its parameters
have no consistent meaning.

<Callout title="Stationary does not mean boring">
  <p>
    A stationary series can be highly structured — strongly autocorrelated, with rich short-term
    dynamics. What it cannot have is <em>drift</em>: no matter how far you look, the level and the
    spread stay put. That is why differencing works. It removes the drift and leaves the structure
    behind for the model to fit.
  </p>
</Callout>

<h2 id="spotting-it">Spotting non-stationarity</h2>

Three signals, in decreasing order of reliability.

**Plot it.** A visible trend or a widening band is non-stationarity, and you rarely need anything
else to see it.

**Compare windows.** Split the series into segments and compute the mean and variance of each. If
they drift, so does the series.

**Look at the autocorrelation.** A stationary series has an ACF that decays to zero quickly. A
non-stationary one has an ACF that stays high across many lags, because every point is close to
its neighbours simply by virtue of the shared trend.

<h2 id="adf-test">The augmented Dickey-Fuller test</h2>

The ADF test formalises the question, with a null hypothesis that is the opposite way round from
what most people first assume:

- $H_0$: the series **has a unit root** — it is non-stationary.
- $H_1$: the series is stationary.

So a **small p-value rejects non-stationarity**, which is the outcome you want. A large p-value
means you failed to reject non-stationarity — which is not the same as proving stationarity, but
in practice is treated as "difference it."

<PythonCell label="ADF, before and after differencing">
{`from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller

def report_adf(name, series):
    statistic, p_value, lags, n_obs, critical, _ = adfuller(series.dropna())
    verdict = "stationary" if p_value < 0.05 else "NON-stationary"
    print(f"{name}")
    print(f"   ADF statistic {statistic:9.4f}   p-value {p_value:.4f}   -> {verdict}")
    print(f"   critical values: " +
          "  ".join(f"{k} {v:.3f}" for k, v in sorted(critical.items())))

report_adf("original series", sessions)
print()
report_adf("first difference   (d = 1)", sessions.diff())
print()
report_adf("seasonal difference (D = 1, period 7)", sessions.diff(7))
print()
report_adf("both differences", sessions.diff().diff(7))`}
</PythonCell>

The original series fails decisively. Every differenced version passes, which gives you a choice —
and the choice matters, because differencing is not free.

<h2 id="differencing">Differencing</h2>

**Ordinary differencing** replaces each value with its change from the previous one:

$$
\nabla y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}
$$

This removes a linear trend. Differencing twice removes a quadratic one, though needing $d = 2$ is
uncommon and usually a hint that the series should be transformed instead.

**Seasonal differencing** subtracts the value one full period back:

$$
\nabla_m y_t = y_t - y_{t-m}
$$

With $m = 7$ this compares each day to the same weekday last week, removing both the weekly
pattern and — because the trend over one week is small — much of the trend as well.

<Callout title="Over-differencing is a real error" variant="pitfall">
  <p>
    Each difference costs an observation and injects artificial negative autocorrelation at lag 1.
    Differencing an already-stationary series makes the model harder to fit and the forecasts
    worse. The rule is to use the <em>smallest</em> d and D that achieve stationarity — usually 0
    or 1 each, essentially never more than 1 seasonal difference.
  </p>
</Callout>

<PythonCell label="Choosing d and D, and seeing over-differencing">
{`from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import acf

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(9.5, 5.5))
series_to_plot = [
    ("original", sessions),
    ("first difference", sessions.diff()),
    ("seasonal difference (7)", sessions.diff(7)),
    ("differenced twice over-eagerly", sessions.diff().diff().diff(7)),
]

for ax, (name, series) in zip(axes.ravel(), series_to_plot):
    clean = series.dropna()
    ax.plot(clean.index, clean.values, linewidth=0.5)
    ax.set_title(f"{name}  (sd {clean.std():,.0f})", fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout()

print(f"{'series':<34}{'sd':>12}{'lag-1 ACF':>12}")
for name, series in series_to_plot:
    clean = series.dropna()
    lag1 = acf(clean, nlags=1)[1]
    print(f"{name:<34}{clean.std():>12,.0f}{lag1:>12.4f}")

print()
print("Note the last row: the standard deviation goes UP and the lag-1")
print("autocorrelation swings strongly negative. Both are signatures of")
print("over-differencing - the transform is adding noise, not removing trend.")

# statsmodels can suggest the order of differencing directly.
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import kpss
statistic, p_value, _, _ = kpss(sessions.diff(7).dropna(), regression="c", nlags="auto")
print()
print("KPSS has the OPPOSITE null (H0 = stationary):")
print(f"  seasonal difference: statistic {statistic:.4f}, p {p_value:.4f} -> "
      f"{'stationary' if p_value > 0.05 else 'non-stationary'}")
print("  Agreement between ADF and KPSS is stronger evidence than either alone.")`}
</PythonCell>

Running ADF and KPSS together is good practice precisely because their nulls are opposite. ADF
rejecting *and* KPSS failing to reject is consistent evidence for stationarity; the two
disagreeing means the series is ambiguous and worth more thought.

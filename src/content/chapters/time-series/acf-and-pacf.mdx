<h2 id="autocorrelation">Autocorrelation</h2>

The **autocorrelation function** measures how correlated a series is with itself $k$ steps back:

$$
\rho_k = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(y_t,\, y_{t-k})}{\operatorname{Var}(y_t)}
$$

$\rho_0$ is always 1. After that the values tell you how long the series remembers. A slow decay
means long memory — or, more often, a trend that has not been differenced away. A sharp cutoff
after lag $q$ is the signature of a **moving average** process of order $q$.

<h2 id="partial">Partial autocorrelation</h2>

The ACF has a blind spot. If today depends on yesterday and yesterday on the day before, then
today correlates with two days ago *through* yesterday — even with no direct relationship. The
ACF cannot distinguish direct influence from inherited influence.

The **partial** autocorrelation at lag $k$ measures the correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-k}$
**after removing the effect of all the intervening lags**. It is exactly the "holding the others
fixed" idea from multiple regression, applied to a series' own past.

A sharp cutoff in the PACF after lag $p$ is the signature of an **autoregressive** process of
order $p$.

<h2 id="reading-the-plots">Reading the plots</h2>

The classic identification table:

| | ACF | PACF |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **AR(p)** | Decays gradually | **Cuts off after lag p** |
| **MA(q)** | **Cuts off after lag q** | Decays gradually |
| **ARMA(p,q)** | Decays | Decays |
| **Non-stationary** | Stays high for many lags | Large spike at lag 1 |

The shaded band on the plots is a 95% confidence interval around zero, roughly $\pm 1.96/\sqrt{n}$.
Spikes inside it are consistent with noise.

<Callout title="Expect false positives" variant="warning">
  <p>
    At the 95% level, about one lag in twenty will poke outside the band by chance. On a 30-lag
    plot that is one or two spikes you should ignore. Treat an isolated spike at lag 17 as noise
    unless you have a reason to expect structure there; treat a spike at the seasonal period as
    real.
  </p>
</Callout>

import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_acf, plot_pacf

traffic = pd.read_csv("web-traffic.csv", parse_dates=["date"]).set_index("date")
traffic.index.freq = "D"
sessions = traffic["sessions"]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(9.5, 5.8))

plot_acf(sessions, lags=30, ax=axes[0, 0])
axes[0, 0].set_title("ACF - original (non-stationary)")
plot_pacf(sessions, lags=30, ax=axes[0, 1], method="ywm")
axes[0, 1].set_title("PACF - original")

seasonal_diff = sessions.diff(7).dropna()
plot_acf(seasonal_diff, lags=30, ax=axes[1, 0])
axes[1, 0].set_title("ACF - after seasonal differencing")
plot_pacf(seasonal_diff, lags=30, ax=axes[1, 1], method="ywm")
axes[1, 1].set_title("PACF - after seasonal differencing")

plt.tight_layout()

print("The top row is the picture of a non-stationary series: autocorrelation")
print("stays high for thirty lags because every point shares the same trend.")`}

The reading is clean here: after seasonal differencing, lags 1 through 6 sit inside the band, and
lag 7 is a large negative spike in both ACF and PACF. That points at a seasonal moving-average
term with $s = 7$, and essentially no non-seasonal structure — a strong hint toward the model
fitted in the next lesson.

<Callout title="ACF and PACF suggest; they do not decide" variant="warning">
  <p>
    Textbook ACF/PACF patterns are clean because textbook data is simulated from a known process.
    Real plots are ambiguous more often than not. Use them to narrow the candidates to two or
    three, then choose between those with AIC and residual diagnostics — which is exactly how the
    next lesson proceeds.
  </p>
</Callout>

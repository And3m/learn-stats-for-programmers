<h2 id="the-idea">The idea</h2>

Monte Carlo simulation replaces a calculation you cannot do with an experiment you can. Instead of
deriving the distribution of an outcome, you generate that outcome thousands of times from random
inputs and look at what you get.

It is named after the casino in Monaco, and it earned its place in serious work on the Manhattan
Project, where the neutron-diffusion problems were analytically hopeless but straightforward to
sample.

The procedure is always the same three steps:

1. Build a model that turns random inputs into an outcome.
2. Run it many times with inputs drawn from their distributions.
3. Study the distribution of outcomes.

The output is not a number. It is a **distribution** — which is the honest form of an answer under
uncertainty, and the reason simulation is often preferable to a point estimate even when a
closed-form solution exists.

<h2 id="when-to-reach-for-it">When to reach for it</h2>

Simulation earns its keep when any of the following hold.

**The algebra is intractable.** The distribution of a sum of independent normals is a normal, and
you can write it down. The distribution of a *maximum* of correlated lognormals passed through a
piecewise payoff function is not something you will derive.

**The problem has path dependence.** A project's completion time depends on which tasks slipped
and in what order; an inventory policy depends on the sequence of demands. Chapter&nbsp;13's
project risk analysis and chapter&nbsp;14's process behaviour are both of this kind.

**You need a full distribution, not an average.** "Expected profit is £2m" is much less useful
than "there is a 12% chance of losing money." Tail probabilities are exactly what simulation is
good at and what averages hide.

**The model is a black box.** If the outcome comes out of a solver, a spreadsheet or a piece of
code, you can still sample its inputs — as chapter&nbsp;8's scenario analysis did to a linear
program.

<Callout title="Simulation is not a substitute for understanding" variant="warning">
  A simulation is only as good as the model inside it. If you assume independence between inputs
  that actually move together, or use a normal distribution for a quantity with a fat tail, the
  simulation will produce a precise, confident and wrong answer — and its apparent sophistication
  makes the error harder to spot than it would be in a one-line formula.
</Callout>

<h2 id="law-of-large-numbers">The law of large numbers</h2>

Why is sampling legitimate? Because of the law of large numbers: as the number of independent
trials grows, the sample mean converges to the true expected value.

$$
\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \;\longrightarrow\; E[X] \quad \text{as } n \to \infty
$$

The central limit theorem sharpens this into something operational. The standard error of the
estimate is

$$
SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

The $\sqrt{n}$ is the crucial and slightly disappointing part. Halving your error requires
**four times** as many trials. Getting one more decimal digit requires a hundred times as many.
Simulation converges reliably but slowly, which is why the question "how many trials?" deserves a
real answer rather than a round number.

<PythonCell label="Convergence, and how slow it is">
{`import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

# A quantity with no convenient closed form: the maximum of three
# correlated delivery times, each lognormal.
def simulate(n):
    base = rng.normal(0, 1, n)                     # a shared latent factor
    times = np.empty((n, 3))
    for k in range(3):
        idiosyncratic = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
        times[:, k] = np.exp(1.6 + 0.35 * (0.6 * base + 0.8 * idiosyncratic))
    return times.max(axis=1)                        # the slowest of the three

sample = simulate(200_000)
truth = sample.mean()
print(f"reference estimate from 200,000 trials: {truth:.4f} days")
print()

print(f"{'trials':>10}{'estimate':>12}{'error':>10}{'SE':>10}")
for n in [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000]:
    subsample = sample[:n]
    estimate = subsample.mean()
    se = subsample.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
    print(f"{n:>10,}{estimate:>12.4f}{abs(estimate - truth):>10.4f}{se:>10.4f}")

print()
print("Each 100x increase in trials buys one decimal place. That is the")
print("sqrt(n) rate, and it is why simulation is used where algebra fails")
print("rather than where algebra is merely tedious.")

running = np.cumsum(sample) / np.arange(1, len(sample) + 1)
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(np.arange(1, 20_001), running[:20_000], linewidth=1)
ax.axhline(truth, linewidth=1.5)
ax.set_xscale("log")
ax.set_xlabel("trials")
ax.set_ylabel("running mean")
ax.set_title("The running mean settles - slowly")`}
</PythonCell>

<PythonCell label="How many trials do you actually need?">
{`# Work backwards from a precision target.
sigma = sample.std(ddof=1)
print(f"sample standard deviation: {sigma:.4f}")
print()
print(f"{'target +/- (95%)':>18}{'trials needed':>16}")
for tolerance in [0.5, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01]:
    needed = (1.96 * sigma / tolerance) ** 2
    print(f"{tolerance:>18.2f}{needed:>16,.0f}")

print()
print("Estimating a probability rather than a mean is easier, because the")
print("variance of an indicator is bounded by 0.25:")
print(f"{'target +/- (95%)':>18}{'trials needed':>16}")
for tolerance in [0.05, 0.02, 0.01, 0.005, 0.001]:
    needed = (1.96 ** 2) * 0.25 / tolerance ** 2
    print(f"{tolerance:>18.3f}{needed:>16,.0f}")

print()
# Tail probabilities are the hard case: rare events need many trials
# simply to be observed a reasonable number of times.
print("Trials needed to see a rare event about 100 times:")
for probability in [0.1, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001]:
    print(f"  P = {probability:<8} -> {100 / probability:>12,.0f} trials")

for threshold in [10, 15, 20]:
    hits = (sample > threshold).mean()
    print()
    print(f"P(slowest delivery > {threshold} days) = {hits:.5f} "
          f"({int(hits * len(sample)):,} of {len(sample):,} trials)")`}
</PythonCell>

That last block is the practical warning about tails. Estimating a 1-in-10,000 event needs about a
million trials just to observe it a hundred times — and a hundred observations is a fairly loose
estimate. Rare-event estimation needs variance-reduction techniques (importance sampling,
stratification) that plain Monte Carlo does not provide.

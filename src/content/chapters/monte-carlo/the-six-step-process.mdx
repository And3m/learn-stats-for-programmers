Before automating anything it is worth doing one pass by hand, because the manual version makes
the mechanism obvious and the automated version then reads as an optimisation rather than a
mystery.

<h2 id="the-steps">The six steps</h2>

1. **Establish a probability distribution** for each uncertain input.
2. **Build the cumulative distribution** for each.
3. **Assign an interval of random numbers** to each outcome, sized by its probability.
4. **Generate random numbers.**
5. **Simulate a series of trials**, mapping each random number to an outcome.
6. **Analyse the results.**

Steps 2 and 3 look like historical curiosities — they exist because the method predates computers,
when trials were run with a table of random digits. They are worth understanding anyway, because
they are precisely what `np.random.choice` does internally, and knowing that removes the magic.

<h2 id="by-hand">One pass by hand</h2>

The scenario: a workshop's daily demand for a spare part, observed over 200 days.

<PythonCell label="Steps 1 to 3: distribution, cumulative, intervals">
{`import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# Step 1 - the empirical distribution of daily demand.
demand_values = np.array([0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
observed_days = np.array([20, 40, 60, 44, 26, 10])

probability = observed_days / observed_days.sum()

# Step 2 - the cumulative distribution.
cumulative = np.cumsum(probability)

# Step 3 - a random-number interval per outcome, sized by its probability.
lower = np.concatenate([[0.0], cumulative[:-1]])

table = pd.DataFrame({
    "demand": demand_values,
    "days": observed_days,
    "probability": probability.round(3),
    "cumulative": cumulative.round(3),
    "interval": [f"[{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f})" for lo, hi in zip(lower, cumulative)],
})
print(table.to_string(index=False))

print()
print(f"total days observed: {observed_days.sum()}")
print(f"probabilities sum to: {probability.sum():.10f}")
print(f"expected demand: {(demand_values * probability).sum():.4f} units/day")

print()
print("Each interval's WIDTH equals that outcome's probability. A uniform")
print("random number lands in each interval exactly as often as the outcome")
print("should occur. That is the whole trick.")`}
</PythonCell>

<h2 id="intervals">Random-number intervals</h2>

The mapping in step 3 is **inverse transform sampling**. Draw $u$ uniformly from $[0,1)$ and
return the smallest outcome whose cumulative probability exceeds $u$:

$$
X = F^{-1}(u), \qquad u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)
$$

Because the interval assigned to each outcome has width equal to its probability, a uniform draw
selects each outcome at exactly the right rate. The same idea works for continuous distributions —
it is how `scipy.stats.<dist>.ppf(u)` generates samples.

<PythonCell label="Steps 4 and 5: draw, map, and repeat">
{`rng = np.random.default_rng(23)

def sample_demand(u):
    """Inverse transform: map a uniform draw to a demand value."""
    return demand_values[np.searchsorted(cumulative, u, side="right")]

# Step 4 - generate random numbers.
draws = rng.random(20)

# Step 5 - one trial per draw, done visibly.
print("Twenty trials, spelled out:")
print(f"{'trial':>6}{'random':>10}{'demand':>9}")
for trial, u in enumerate(draws, start=1):
    print(f"{trial:>6}{u:>10.4f}{sample_demand(u):>9d}")

manual = np.array([sample_demand(u) for u in draws])
print()
print(f"mean demand over 20 trials: {manual.mean():.3f}  "
      f"(true expectation {(demand_values * probability).sum():.3f})")
print("Twenty trials is not many. The next cell runs a hundred thousand.")`}
</PythonCell>

<h2 id="analysing">Step 6, and the vectorised version</h2>

Once the mechanism is clear, the loop disappears. NumPy generates all the draws at once and maps
them in a single operation, which is both far faster and far shorter.

<PythonCell label="The same simulation, vectorised">
{`trials = 100_000

# The manual route, vectorised: uniform draws through the inverse CDF.
u = rng.random(trials)
by_hand = demand_values[np.searchsorted(cumulative, u, side="right")]

# What np.random.choice does for you - the identical computation.
built_in = rng.choice(demand_values, size=trials, p=probability)

print(f"{'demand':>8}{'expected %':>13}{'inverse CDF %':>16}{'choice() %':>13}")
for value, p in zip(demand_values, probability):
    print(f"{value:>8}{p * 100:>13.2f}"
          f"{(by_hand == value).mean() * 100:>16.2f}"
          f"{(built_in == value).mean() * 100:>13.2f}")

print()
print(f"mean:  expected {(demand_values * probability).sum():.4f}   "
      f"simulated {by_hand.mean():.4f}")
print(f"sd:    expected "
      f"{np.sqrt((probability * (demand_values - (demand_values * probability).sum()) ** 2).sum()):.4f}   "
      f"simulated {by_hand.std(ddof=1):.4f}")

print()
# Step 6 proper: the questions a distribution can answer that a mean cannot.
print("Questions an average could not have answered:")
print(f"  P(demand = 0)      {(by_hand == 0).mean():.4f}")
print(f"  P(demand >= 4)     {(by_hand >= 4).mean():.4f}")
print(f"  95th percentile    {np.percentile(by_hand, 95):.0f} units")
print()
weekly = by_hand[: (trials // 7) * 7].reshape(-1, 7).sum(axis=1)
print(f"Weekly demand (7 days summed), from {len(weekly):,} simulated weeks:")
print(f"  mean {weekly.mean():.2f}   sd {weekly.std(ddof=1):.2f}")
print(f"  a stock of {np.percentile(weekly, 95):.0f} units covers 95% of weeks")
print(f"  a stock of {np.percentile(weekly, 99):.0f} units covers 99% of weeks")`}
</PythonCell>

The weekly figure is the point of the whole exercise. Expected demand is a little over two units a
day, so a week averages about fifteen — but stocking fifteen leaves you short roughly half the
time. The distribution tells you what the mean cannot, and that gap is why service-level decisions
are made from simulations rather than averages.

<Callout title="Set a seed, and say so">
  <p>
    <code>np.random.default_rng(23)</code> makes a simulation reproducible: the same seed gives the
    same answer, so a colleague can verify your numbers. Report the seed alongside the results, and
    re-run with several different seeds before believing anything — if your conclusion changes with
    the seed, you have not run enough trials.
  </p>
</Callout>

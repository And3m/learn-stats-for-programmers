Discrete simulation samples from a list of outcomes. Continuous simulation samples from a
distribution over a range, and the canonical example — five years of daily closing prices for an
index — is in `index-prices.csv`.

The point of this lesson is as much about what a simulation **cannot** promise as about how to
build one.

<h2 id="log-returns">Log returns</h2>

Price models are built on returns, not prices, and on **log** returns specifically:

$$
r_t = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right)
$$

Three reasons.

**They are additive.** The log return over a period is the sum of the log returns within it, so
multi-day returns are sums rather than products. Simple returns must be compounded.

**They are symmetric.** A simple return of $+50\%$ followed by $-50\%$ leaves you at 75% of where
you started — the pair does not cancel. In logs, $+0.405$ and $-0.405$ cancel exactly.

**They are closer to normal.** Prices are strictly positive and right-skewed; log returns are
roughly symmetric and much better behaved, which makes the normal model at least defensible.

A price cannot go below zero, and modelling the logarithm as a random walk enforces that
automatically.

<PythonCell label="From prices to returns">

<h2 id="parameters">Drift and volatility</h2>

Two parameters summarise the return series:

$$
\mu = \text{mean daily log return}, \qquad \sigma = \text{standard deviation of daily log returns}
$$

Both are conventionally annualised, using 252 trading days:

$$
\mu_{\text{annual}} = 252\,\mu, \qquad \sigma_{\text{annual}} = \sqrt{252}\,\sigma
$$

Note the asymmetry: drift scales with time, volatility with its square root. That difference is
why volatility dominates over short horizons and drift dominates over long ones.

<PythonCell label="Estimating the parameters">
{`daily_drift = log_returns.mean()
daily_vol = log_returns.std(ddof=1)

print(f"daily   drift {daily_drift:>10.6f}   volatility {daily_vol:>10.6f}")
print(f"annual  drift {daily_drift * 252:>10.4f}   volatility "
      f"{daily_vol * np.sqrt(252):>10.4f}")

print()
print("Do the returns look normal? Not exactly:")
from scipy import stats
print(f"  skew     {stats.skew(log_returns):>8.4f}   (0 for a normal)")

      f"{'not normal' if p_value < 0.05 else 'consistent with normal'}")

for year, value in by_year.items():
    print(f"  {year}  annualised vol {value:.4f}  {'#' * int(value * 120)}")

ax.plot(rolling_vol.index, rolling_vol.values, linewidth=1)

ax.set_title("30-day rolling volatility - calm periods and turbulent ones")`}
</PythonCell>

The diagnostics matter. The returns have excess kurtosis — fat tails, meaning extreme days are
more common than a normal predicts — and volatility clusters in time rather than staying constant.
Both violate the assumptions of the model we are about to build, and both mean the simulation will
**understate** extreme outcomes.

<h2 id="simulating-paths">Simulating paths</h2>

Geometric Brownian motion evolves the price as

$$
P_t = P_{t-1} \exp\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)\Delta t + \sigma\,\varepsilon_t\sqrt{\Delta t}\right],
\qquad \varepsilon_t \sim N(0,1)
$$

The $-\sigma^2/2$ term is the convexity correction. Without it the *expected price* would exceed
what the drift implies, because exponentiating a symmetric distribution produces an asymmetric one.

<PythonCell label="A thousand futures">
{`rng = np.random.default_rng(41)

horizon = 252            # one trading year ahead
paths = 5_000
start = prices.iloc[-1]

shocks = rng.normal(
    loc=daily_drift - 0.5 * daily_vol ** 2,
    scale=daily_vol,
    size=(paths, horizon),
)
simulated = start * np.exp(np.cumsum(shocks, axis=1))
final = simulated[:, -1]

print(f"starting price {start:,.2f}, simulating {paths:,} paths {horizon} days ahead")
print()
print(f"  mean final price    {final.mean():>10,.2f}")
print(f"  median final price  {np.median(final):>10,.2f}   <- below the mean; the")
print(f"                                     distribution is right-skewed")
print(f"  5th percentile      {np.percentile(final, 5):>10,.2f}")
print(f"  95th percentile     {np.percentile(final, 95):>10,.2f}")
print()
print(f"  P(price ends lower than today) {np.mean(final < start):>7.2%}")
print(f"  P(price gains more than 20%)   {np.mean(final > start * 1.2):>7.2%}")
print(f"  P(price falls more than 20%)   {np.mean(final < start * 0.8):>7.2%}")

drawdown = simulated.min(axis=1) / start - 1
print(f"  median worst drawdown along the path {np.median(drawdown):>7.2%}")

fig, (left, right) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.5, 3.8))

for row in simulated[:120]:
    left.plot(days, row, linewidth=0.4, alpha=0.5)

    left.plot(days, np.percentile(simulated, percentile, axis=0), linewidth=width)
left.set_xlabel("trading days ahead")
left.set_ylabel("price")

<h2 id="reading-the-fan">Reading the fan honestly</h2>

The fan chart is the most seductive image in quantitative finance, and it is routinely
over-interpreted. What it does and does not say:

**It says**: *if* returns are independent and normally distributed with the drift and volatility
estimated from this history, and *if* those parameters stay constant, then this is the range of
outcomes.

**It does not say** anything about what will actually happen, because every one of those
conditions is false to some degree.

The specific failures, all visible in the diagnostics above:

- **Returns are not normal.** The observed excess kurtosis means real extreme days are more common
  than the model generates, so the tails of the fan are too thin.
- **Volatility is not constant.** It clusters, so real paths have calm stretches and violent ones
  rather than uniform noise.
- **Parameters are estimates.** The drift in particular is estimated with enormous uncertainty —
  far more than most people realise.

<PythonCell label="How badly is the drift known?">
{`n = len(log_returns)
drift_se = daily_vol / np.sqrt(n)

print(f"daily drift estimate {daily_drift:.6f}  +/- {1.96 * drift_se:.6f}  (95%)")
print(f"annualised:          {daily_drift * 252:.4f}  "
      f"[{(daily_drift - 1.96 * drift_se) * 252:.4f}, "
      f"{(daily_drift + 1.96 * drift_se) * 252:.4f}]")
print()
print("That interval spans both solidly positive and negative annual drift,")
print("from five years of daily data. Volatility is estimated far more")
print("precisely than drift - which is why risk models are more trustworthy")
print("than return forecasts.")

print()
print("What the fan looks like under the interval's endpoints:")
for label, drift in [
    ("lower bound", daily_drift - 1.96 * drift_se),
    ("point estimate", daily_drift),
    ("upper bound", daily_drift + 1.96 * drift_se),
]:
    shocks = rng.normal(drift - 0.5 * daily_vol ** 2, daily_vol, size=(3_000, horizon))
    ends = start * np.exp(np.cumsum(shocks, axis=1))[:, -1]
    print(f"  {label:<15} median {np.median(ends):>9,.2f}   "
          f"P(loss) {np.mean(ends < start):>6.2%}")

print()
# Bootstrapping from the actual returns keeps the real fat tails.
bootstrap = rng.choice(log_returns.values, size=(paths, horizon), replace=True)
bootstrap_final = start * np.exp(bootstrap.sum(axis=1))
print("Bootstrapping the historical returns instead of assuming normality:")
print(f"  normal model  5th pct {np.percentile(final, 5):>9,.2f}   "
      f"1st pct {np.percentile(final, 1):>9,.2f}")
print(f"  bootstrapped  5th pct {np.percentile(bootstrap_final, 5):>9,.2f}   "
      f"1st pct {np.percentile(bootstrap_final, 1):>9,.2f}")
print("  The bootstrap keeps the observed fat tails and gives a worse")
print("  extreme case - which is the more honest one.")`}

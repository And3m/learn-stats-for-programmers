Chapter&nbsp;2 introduced the addition and multiplication rules in their simplest forms, which
required mutual exclusivity and independence respectively. This lesson removes both assumptions
and adds the single most useful trick in elementary probability.

<h2 id="complement-rule">The complement rule</h2>

For any event $A$, either $A$ happens or it does not:

$$
P(A) + P(A^{c}) = 1 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad P(A) = 1 - P(A^{c})
$$

Trivially true, and enormously useful, because **"at least one" is almost always harder to compute
directly than "none".**

Suppose a service makes 8 independent calls to a dependency, each failing with probability 0.02.
What is the probability at least one fails? Computing it directly means summing the probabilities
of exactly one failure, exactly two, and so on up to eight — nine terms. From the other side it is
one line:

$$
P(\text{at least one fails}) = 1 - P(\text{none fail}) = 1 - 0.98^{8} \approx 0.1492
$$

Roughly a 15% chance, from components each 98% reliable. That gap between component reliability
and system reliability is one of the most consistently underestimated things in engineering.

<PythonCell label="At least one, from the other side">
{`import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

p_fail = 0.02

print(" calls   P(at least one failure)")
for n in [1, 4, 8, 20, 50, 100]:
    direct = stats.binom.sf(0, n, p_fail)      # 1 - P(X = 0), computed exactly
    complement = 1 - (1 - p_fail) ** n
    print(f"  {n:4d}   {complement:.4f}   (scipy agrees: {direct:.4f})")

print()
print("Component reliability 98% -> system reliability falls fast with fan-out.")

# The classic: how many people before two share a birthday, with probability > 1/2?
def shared_birthday(n):
    days = np.arange(365, 365 - n, -1)
    return 1 - np.prod(days / 365)

for n in [10, 23, 30, 50, 70]:
    print(f"  {n:3d} people -> P(a shared birthday) = {shared_birthday(n):.4f}")`}
</PythonCell>

The birthday result is the standard demonstration that intuition about "at least one" is
unreliable: 23 people is enough to make a shared birthday more likely than not, which almost
nobody guesses.

<h2 id="general-rules">Addition and multiplication, generalised</h2>

Both simple rules are special cases. The general forms always apply.

**General addition rule** — subtract the overlap, always:

$$
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)
$$

When $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, $P(A \cap B) = 0$ and the simple rule reappears.

**General multiplication rule** — the second probability is conditional on the first:

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)
$$

When $A$ and $B$ are independent, $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$ and the simple rule reappears. Note that
this is where **independence gets its actual definition**: two events are independent precisely
when conditioning on one does not change the other.

Drawing two aces from a deck without replacement shows the difference plainly:

$$
P(\text{two aces}) = \frac{4}{52} \times \frac{3}{51} = \frac{12}{2652} \approx 0.00452
$$

The second factor is $3/51$, not $4/52$, because one ace is gone and the deck is smaller.

<h2 id="quick-reference">A quick reference table</h2>

| The question says | Rule | Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "A **and** B", independent | Multiplication | $P(A)P(B)$ |
| "A **and** B", in general | General multiplication | $P(A)P(B \mid A)$ |
| "A **or** B", mutually exclusive | Addition | $P(A) + P(B)$ |
| "A **or** B", in general | General addition | $P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$ |
| "**at least one**" | Complement | $1 - P(\text{none})$ |
| "**none**" | Multiplication over complements | $\prod (1 - p_i)$ |
| "**given that**" | Conditional | $P(A \cap B) / P(B)$ |

The words in bold are the trigger. Training yourself to notice them is most of the skill.

from scipy import stats
import math

print("1) A deploy pipeline has 6 independent stages, each 97% reliable.")

print(f"   via combinations:       C(4,2)/C(52,2)  = {math.comb(4,2)/math.comb(52,2):.5f}")

print()
print("3) One card. P(red or face card)?")
p_red, p_face, p_both = 26/52, 12/52, 6/52
print(f"   general addition: {p_red:.4f} + {p_face:.4f} - {p_both:.4f} = {p_red + p_face - p_both:.4f}")

print()
print("4) 200 units, 3% defective. P(at least 1 defective in a sample of 25)?")
print(f"   complement: 1 - 0.97^25 = {1 - 0.97 ** 25:.4f}")

print()
print("5) Two dice. P(sum is 8 | first die shows 3)?")
print("   Given the first is 3, only the second matters, and it must be 5.")
print(f"   P = 1/6 = {1/6:.4f}")
print("   Note P(sum is 8) unconditionally = 5/36 =", round(5/36, 4),
      "- conditioning changed it, so these events are not independent.")`}
</PythonCell>

That last example is the bridge to the next lesson. Conditioning on the first die changed the
probability of the sum, which is exactly what it means for two events to be dependent — and
handling that properly is what conditional probability is for.

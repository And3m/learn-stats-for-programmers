<h2 id="the-idea">Narrowing the sample space</h2>

The conditional probability of $A$ **given** $B$ is

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0
$$

What the "given" does is **shrink the world**. You are no longer asking about all possible
outcomes; you are asking about the outcomes in which $B$ occurred, and asking what fraction of
those also have $A$. The denominator changes from 1 to $P(B)$, and everything else follows.

Roll two dice. Unconditionally, $P(\text{sum} = 8) = 5/36$. Now condition on the first die showing
a 3: the world has shrunk from 36 outcomes to 6, and exactly one of those six gives a sum of 8. So
$P(\text{sum} = 8 \mid \text{first} = 3) = 1/6$. The condition changed the answer, which is
precisely what dependence means.

<Callout title="The most common error in the subject" variant="pitfall">
  <p>
    <strong>P(A | B) is not P(B | A).</strong> The probability that someone is coughing given they
    have a cold is very high. The probability that someone has a cold given they are coughing is
    much lower. Confusing the two — the "prosecutor's fallacy" — has produced wrongful
    convictions, bad medical decisions and a great many misread dashboards.
  </p>
</Callout>

<h2 id="independence">Conditional probability and independence</h2>

Independence now has a precise test rather than a vibe. Events $A$ and $B$ are independent if and
only if

$$
P(A \mid B) = P(A) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)
$$

In words: knowing $B$ happened tells you nothing new about $A$. Checking this on real data is a
one-line comparison, and doing it is a good habit — assumed independence is behind a large
fraction of underestimated risks.

<PythonCell label="Testing independence directly">
{`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(17)
n = 400_000

# Two dice.
d1 = rng.integers(1, 7, n)
d2 = rng.integers(1, 7, n)

a = (d1 + d2) == 8          # A: the sum is 8
b = d1 == 3                 # B: the first die is a 3
c = d2 == 5                 # C: the second die is a 5

def report(name, x, y):
    p_x, p_y = x.mean(), y.mean()
    p_joint = (x & y).mean()
    p_cond = p_joint / p_y
    verdict = "independent" if abs(p_joint - p_x * p_y) < 0.002 else "DEPENDENT"
    print(f"{name}")
    print(f"   P(A)        = {p_x:.4f}")
    print(f"   P(A | B)    = {p_cond:.4f}")
    print(f"   P(A)P(B)    = {p_x * p_y:.4f}   P(A and B) = {p_joint:.4f}   -> {verdict}")

report("sum=8  vs  first die = 3", a, b)
print()
report("second die = 5  vs  first die = 3", c, b)`}
</PythonCell>

The first pair is dependent — knowing the first die is a 3 raises the chance of a sum of 8 from
$5/36 \approx 0.139$ to $1/6 \approx 0.167$. The second pair is independent, as two separate dice
must be.

<h2 id="natural-frequencies">The natural-frequency shortcut</h2>

Here is the classic problem. A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test detects it correctly 99%
of the time, and has a 5% false-positive rate. **You test positive. What is the probability you
have the disease?**

Most people, including most doctors when asked, answer somewhere between 95% and 99%. The correct
answer is about 2%.

The reason the correct answer is so hard to feel is that it depends on a base rate the question
mentions only in passing. Restate the problem in **counts** rather than probabilities and it
becomes almost obvious:

Imagine 100,000 people.

- **100** actually have the disease (1 in 1,000).
  - Of those, **99** test positive (99% sensitivity).
- **99,900** do not have it.
  - Of those, **4,995** test positive anyway (5% false positive rate).

So $99 + 4995 = 5094$ people test positive, and only 99 of them are ill:

$$
P(\text{ill} \mid \text{positive}) = \frac{99}{5094} \approx 0.0194
$$

The false positives swamp the true positives, because there are a thousand times more healthy
people to draw them from. Nothing about the test is bad; the base rate is simply doing most of
the work.

<PythonCell label="Natural frequencies, and what changes the answer">
{`prevalence = 0.001
sensitivity = 0.99          # P(positive | ill)
false_positive = 0.05       # P(positive | healthy)

population = 100_000
ill = population * prevalence
healthy = population - ill

true_positives = ill * sensitivity
false_positives = healthy * false_positive
all_positives = true_positives + false_positives

print(f"population        {population:>10,.0f}")
print(f"  ill            {ill:>10,.0f}   -> {true_positives:>8,.0f} test positive")
print(f"  healthy        {healthy:>10,.0f}   -> {false_positives:>8,.0f} test positive")
print(f"  total positive {all_positives:>10,.0f}")
print()
print(f"P(ill | positive) = {true_positives / all_positives:.4f}")

print()
print("The base rate does almost all the work:")
for rate in [0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.20, 0.50]:
    tp = rate * sensitivity
    fp = (1 - rate) * false_positive
    print(f"  prevalence {rate:5.1%}  ->  P(ill | positive) = {tp / (tp + fp):.4f}")`}
</PythonCell>

Screening a general population and testing someone who already has symptoms are therefore
completely different problems, even with an identical test. That is not a statistical subtlety —
it is the whole reason targeted testing works and indiscriminate screening often does not.

<h2 id="bayes">Bayes' theorem</h2>

The formula behind the counting above follows from the definition of conditional probability
applied in both directions. Since $P(A \cap B)$ can be written as either $P(A \mid B)P(B)$ or
$P(B \mid A)P(A)$, setting them equal and rearranging gives

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

and expanding the denominator over all the ways $B$ can happen gives the form you actually use:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid A^{c})\,P(A^{c})}
$$

The vocabulary is worth knowing because it recurs: $P(A)$ is the **prior** (what you believed
before), $P(B \mid A)$ is the **likelihood** (how well the evidence fits the hypothesis), and
$P(A \mid B)$ is the **posterior** (what you should believe now).

<PythonCell label="Bayes, and updating twice">
{`def bayes(prior, likelihood, false_alarm):
    """P(hypothesis | evidence) from prior, P(E|H) and P(E|not H)."""
    numerator = likelihood * prior
    return numerator / (numerator + false_alarm * (1 - prior))

prior = 0.001
posterior = bayes(prior, 0.99, 0.05)
print(f"after one positive test:  {posterior:.4f}")

# A second, independent positive test uses the first posterior as its prior.
second = bayes(posterior, 0.99, 0.05)
print(f"after a second positive:  {second:.4f}")
third = bayes(second, 0.99, 0.05)
print(f"after a third positive:   {third:.4f}")

print()
print("A negative result updates in the other direction:")
negative = bayes(prior, 1 - 0.99, 1 - 0.05)   # P(negative | ill), P(negative | healthy)
print(f"after one negative test:  {negative:.6f}")`}
</PythonCell>

Two independent positive tests take you from 2% to about 28%; three take you past 88%. This is the
practical logic of confirmatory testing, and it is the same updating machinery that sits behind
spam filters, medical triage and A/B test interpretation.

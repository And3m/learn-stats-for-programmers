The normal distribution is the one everybody has seen and rather fewer can use precisely. It
earns its place for a specific reason: the **central limit theorem** says that sums and averages
of many independent contributions tend toward a normal shape, whatever the shape of the individual
contributions. Since a great many measured quantities are the accumulation of many small
independent effects, the bell curve turns up constantly.

<h2 id="shape">Shape and parameters</h2>

A normal distribution is fully specified by two numbers: its mean $\mu$ and its standard
deviation $\sigma$. The density is

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}
$$

You will almost never evaluate that by hand, but its structure is worth reading. The exponent
contains $(x-\mu)/\sigma$ squared — so the density depends on $x$ only through *how many standard
deviations it sits from the mean*. Everything else is a normalising constant that makes the total
area equal 1.

Three properties follow:

- It is **symmetric** about $\mu$, so mean, median and mode coincide.
- $\mu$ **shifts** the curve; $\sigma$ **stretches** it. Changing $\sigma$ does not move the
  centre, and changing $\mu$ does not change the width.
- The tails extend forever in both directions. A normal model always assigns some probability to
  absurd values — negative heights, negative latencies — which is one reason it is the wrong model
  for strictly positive, skewed quantities.

<Callout title="When the normal model is wrong" variant="warning">
  <p>
    Latency, income, file sizes and time-to-failure are all right-skewed and bounded below by
    zero. Fitting a normal to them will place probability on negative values and badly understate
    the upper tail. The usual fix is to model the <em>logarithm</em> as normal — the lognormal
    distribution used in chapter&nbsp;2's latency example, and again in chapter&nbsp;9 for prices.
  </p>
</Callout>

<h2 id="z-scores">Standardising with z-scores</h2>

Because the density depends only on distance-in-standard-deviations, every normal question can be
converted into a question about the **standard normal** — the one with $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$.
The conversion is the z-score:

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

A z-score of $+1.5$ means "one and a half standard deviations above the mean," and that statement
carries the same probabilistic meaning regardless of the units involved. This is what makes
z-scores useful for comparing across scales: a component 2.3 standard deviations above its target
weight and a delivery 2.3 standard deviations later than its target date are equally unusual, even
though one is grams and the other days.

Historically you would look the resulting z up in a table. That table is now a function call, but
the reasoning is unchanged.

<h2 id="empirical-rule">The 68-95-99.7 rule</h2>

For any normal distribution, roughly:

| Within | Contains | Outside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mu \pm 1\sigma$ | 68.3% | about 1 in 3 |
| $\mu \pm 2\sigma$ | 95.4% | about 1 in 22 |
| $\mu \pm 3\sigma$ | 99.7% | about 1 in 370 |

This is worth memorising, because it converts a z-score into a rough probability instantly. It is
also the origin of the three-sigma control limits in chapter&nbsp;14: if a process is stable and
roughly normal, a point beyond three sigma happens about once in 370 samples by chance, which is
rare enough to be worth investigating and common enough not to drown you in false alarms.

<PythonCell label="The normal distribution, seen three ways">
{`import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

# Component weights: target 500 g, process sigma 4 g.
mu, sigma = 500.0, 4.0
weights = stats.norm(loc=mu, scale=sigma)

x = np.linspace(mu - 4 * sigma, mu + 4 * sigma, 500)

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(x, weights.pdf(x))
for k, alpha in [(1, 0.35), (2, 0.22), (3, 0.12)]:
    band = (x >= mu - k * sigma) & (x <= mu + k * sigma)
    ax.fill_between(x[band], weights.pdf(x[band]), alpha=alpha)
ax.set_xlabel("weight (g)")
ax.set_ylabel("density")
ax.set_title("68 / 95 / 99.7")

for k in (1, 2, 3):
    inside = weights.cdf(mu + k * sigma) - weights.cdf(mu - k * sigma)
    print(f"within {k} sigma: {inside:.4%}   outside: 1 in {1 / (1 - inside):.0f}")`}
</PythonCell>

<PythonCell label="Answering real questions">
{`# Continuing with the same distribution.
print(f"P(weight < 494 g)        = {weights.cdf(494):.4f}")
print(f"P(weight > 508 g)        = {1 - weights.cdf(508):.4f}")
print(f"P(496 g < weight < 504 g) = {weights.cdf(504) - weights.cdf(496):.4f}")

print()
# The inverse question: what cutoff leaves 2.5% in each tail?
low, high = weights.ppf(0.025), weights.ppf(0.975)
print(f"middle 95% runs from {low:.2f} g to {high:.2f} g")
print(f"which is mu +/- {(high - mu) / sigma:.3f} sigma  <- the famous 1.96")

print()
# Standardising by hand gives the same answer as scipy.
x_value = 507.5
z = (x_value - mu) / sigma
print(f"z-score of {x_value} g = {z:.3f}")
print(f"P(X > {x_value}) via z    = {1 - stats.norm.cdf(z):.5f}")
print(f"P(X > {x_value}) directly = {1 - weights.cdf(x_value):.5f}")`}
</PythonCell>

That $1.96$ is worth recognising on sight. It is the z-score cutting off 2.5% in each tail, and it
is why 95% confidence intervals in chapter&nbsp;4 are built as "estimate $\pm$ 1.96 standard
errors."

The binomial distribution answers one question: **in $n$ independent attempts each succeeding
with probability $p$, how many succeed?** It is the workhorse for anything countable out of a
fixed total — defective units in a batch, conversions out of visitors, retries that succeed.

<h2 id="the-setup">Four conditions</h2>

A situation is binomial only if all four of these hold:

1. **A fixed number of trials, $n$.** Decided before you start, not by when you stop.
2. **Two outcomes per trial.** Success and failure, however you choose to define them.
3. **Constant probability $p$.** The same on every trial.
4. **Independence.** The outcome of one trial does not affect any other.

These are not pedantic. Inspecting 20 items from a batch of 25 *without replacement* violates
condition 3, because $p$ shifts as you draw — that is a hypergeometric situation, not a binomial
one. Counting how many attempts until the first success violates condition 1 — that is geometric.
And correlated failures violate condition 4, which is the same trap as chapter&nbsp;2's servers in
one rack.

<Callout title="A useful rule of thumb">
  Sampling without replacement is <em>approximately</em> binomial when the sample is small
  relative to the population — a common rule is under 10%. Inspecting 20 units from a production
  run of 50,000 is fine to treat as binomial; inspecting 20 out of 25 is not.
</Callout>

<h2 id="the-formula">The formula, and where it comes from</h2>

The probability of exactly $k$ successes in $n$ trials is

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k}\, p^{k}\, (1-p)^{\,n-k}
$$

Every piece has a plain reading, and it is worth taking them one at a time:

- $p^{k}$ — the probability that $k$ specific trials all succeed, by the multiplication rule for
  independent events.
- $(1-p)^{n-k}$ — the probability the other $n-k$ trials all fail.
- $\binom{n}{k}$ — the number of *different ways* to choose which $k$ of the $n$ trials are the
  successful ones. This is chapter&nbsp;2's combination formula, doing exactly the job it was
  introduced for: order within the sequence does not matter, only which trials succeeded.

Multiply the probability of one specific arrangement by the number of arrangements, and you have
the probability of $k$ successes however they fall.

The mean and variance are pleasingly simple:

$$
E[X] = np, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1-p)
$$

Twenty flips of a fair coin: expect $20 \times 0.5 = 10$ heads, with variance $5$ and standard
deviation about $2.24$.

<h2 id="shape">Mean, variance and shape</h2>

The shape depends on $p$. At $p = 0.5$ the distribution is symmetric. As $p$ moves toward 0 or 1
it becomes skewed, because the count is squeezed against a boundary. And as $n$ grows the whole
thing approaches a normal shape — the central limit theorem again, since a binomial count is
literally a sum of $n$ independent 0/1 trials.

<PythonCell label="Shape across p and n">
{`import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(9.5, 3.2))

for ax, (n, p) in zip(axes, [(20, 0.5), (20, 0.1), (200, 0.1)]):
    k = np.arange(0, n + 1)
    ax.bar(k, stats.binom.pmf(k, n, p), width=0.85)
    ax.set_title(f"n = {n}, p = {p}")
    ax.set_xlabel("successes")
    # Zoom to where the mass actually is.
    mean, sd = n * p, np.sqrt(n * p * (1 - p))
    ax.set_xlim(max(-0.5, mean - 4 * sd), mean + 4 * sd)

axes[0].set_ylabel("P(X = k)")
plt.tight_layout()

for n, p in [(20, 0.5), (20, 0.1), (200, 0.1)]:
    print(f"n={n:3d} p={p:.1f}   mean={n*p:6.1f}   sd={np.sqrt(n*p*(1-p)):5.2f}")`}
</PythonCell>

The third panel is visibly bell-shaped. That is the justification for the normal approximation to
the binomial, which is usually considered safe when both $np \ge 10$ and $n(1-p) \ge 10$.

<h2 id="in-python">Binomial questions in Python</h2>

Three functions cover essentially every binomial question: `pmf` for exactly, `cdf` for at most,
and the complement of `cdf` for at least.

<PythonCell label="Exactly, at most, at least">
{`import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# A batch of 200 units from a process with a 3% defect rate.
n, p = 200, 0.03
batch = stats.binom(n, p)

print(f"expected defectives: {batch.mean():.1f}  (sd {batch.std():.2f})")
print()
print(f"P(exactly 6 defective) = {batch.pmf(6):.4f}")
print(f"P(at most 6)           = {batch.cdf(6):.4f}")
print(f"P(at least 10)         = {batch.sf(9):.4f}   <- sf(9) = 1 - cdf(9)")
print(f"P(none at all)         = {batch.pmf(0):.4f}")

print()
# Acceptance sampling: reject the batch if more than k defectives appear.
# What threshold rejects a good batch less than 5% of the time?
threshold = batch.ppf(0.95)
print(f"reject above {threshold:.0f} defectives -> false-rejection rate "
      f"{batch.sf(threshold):.4f}")

print()
# The four conditions, made concrete: with only 25 units in the population,
# sampling 20 without replacement is hypergeometric, not binomial.
hyper = stats.hypergeom(M=25, n=3, N=20)   # 25 units, 3 defective, sample 20
small = stats.binom(20, 3 / 25)
print(f"P(exactly 3 defective), hypergeometric = {hyper.pmf(3):.4f}")
print(f"P(exactly 3 defective), binomial       = {small.pmf(3):.4f}   <- wrong model")`}
</PythonCell>

That last comparison is the point of the four conditions. The binomial answer is not slightly off;
it is a different number, because the model does not describe the experiment.

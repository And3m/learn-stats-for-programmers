<h2 id="the-surprise">The surprise</h2>

Take a large collection of naturally occurring numbers — river lengths, town populations, invoice
amounts, physical constants — and look at the **first digit** of each.

Intuition says the nine possible leading digits should appear about 11% of the time each. They do
not. The digit 1 leads about **30%** of the time, and 9 only about **4.6%**.

This is Benford's law, first noticed by the astronomer Simon Newcomb in 1881 — he observed that the
early pages of logarithm tables were more worn than the later ones — and rediscovered and
popularised by Frank Benford in 1938.

<h2 id="the-formula">The formula</h2>

The probability that the leading digit is $d$ is

$$
P(D_1 = d) = \log_{10}\!\left(1 + \frac{1}{d}\right), \qquad d \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 9\}
$$

Working through the values:

| Digit | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Frequency | 30.1% | 17.6% | 12.5% | 9.7% | 7.9% | 6.7% | 5.8% | 5.1% | 4.6% |

The first three digits account for 60% of all observations. The law extends to later digits too,
though the effect fades quickly — by the third digit the distribution is nearly uniform.

<PythonCell label="The law, and a first look at real-ish data">
{`import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

digits = np.arange(1, 10)

<h2 id="why-it-happens">Why it happens</h2>

The cleanest argument is **scale invariance**.

Suppose there is a universal law governing leading digits of naturally occurring quantities. It
cannot depend on the units, because units are an arbitrary human choice — a distribution of river
lengths must obey the same law whether measured in kilometres or miles. Converting units
multiplies every value by a constant, which is a shift along the logarithmic axis.

The only distribution of leading digits invariant under such a shift is Benford's.

The mechanism becomes concrete if you think about **the mantissa** — the fractional part of
$\log_{10}$ of a value. Benford's law is exactly the statement that the mantissa is uniformly
distributed on $[0,1)$. Because a logarithmic scale devotes more width to the span from 1 to 2
than from 9 to 10, a uniformly distributed mantissa spends more time with a leading 1.

$$
\log_{10} 2 - \log_{10} 1 = 0.301
\qquad\text{versus}\qquad
\log_{10} 10 - \log_{10} 9 = 0.046
$$

Those are exactly the predicted frequencies.

<PythonCell label="Uniform on the log scale means Benford on the digits">
{`rng = np.random.default_rng(97)

def leading_digit(values):
    values = np.abs(np.asarray(values, dtype=float))
    values = values[values > 0]
    return (values / 10 ** np.floor(np.log10(values))).astype(int)

# Draw the mantissa uniformly - the definition of Benford conformity.
mantissa = rng.random(200_000)
log_uniform = 10 ** (mantissa + rng.integers(1, 6, 200_000))

observed = np.array([(leading_digit(log_uniform) == d).mean() for d in digits])

print(f"{'digit':>6}{'observed':>11}{'Benford':>11}{'difference':>12}")
for digit, seen, expected in zip(digits, observed, benford):
    print(f"{digit:>6}{seen:>11.4f}{expected:>11.4f}{seen - expected:>+12.5f}")

print()
print("Now the other direction: check that real logarithms are uniform.")
values = 10 ** (rng.random(200_000) * 5 + 1)
mantissas = np.log10(values) % 1
print(f"  mean of the mantissa      {mantissas.mean():.4f}   (0.5 if uniform)")
print(f"  standard deviation        {mantissas.std():.4f}   (0.2887 if uniform)")

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.hist(mantissas, bins=50)
ax.axhline(len(mantissas) / 50, linewidth=2)
ax.set_xlabel("fractional part of log10(value)")
ax.set_ylabel("count")
ax.set_title("A uniform mantissa is exactly what Benford conformity means")`}
</PythonCell>

<PythonCell label="Scale invariance, demonstrated">
{`# Convert the same values into different units and re-check the digits.
base = 10 ** (rng.random(100_000) * 4 + 1)

conversions = {
    "original":            1.0,
    "x 2.54 (in -> cm)":   2.54,
    "x 1.609 (mi -> km)":  1.609,
    "x 0.0254":            0.0254,
    "x 1000":              1000.0,
    "x 7.3 (arbitrary)":   7.3,
}

print(f"{'units':<22}" + "".join(f"{d:>7}" for d in digits))
print(f"{'Benford predicts':<22}" + "".join(f"{p:>7.3f}" for p in benford))
print("-" * (22 + 7 * 9))
for label, factor in conversions.items():
    frequencies = [(leading_digit(base * factor) == d).mean() for d in digits]
    print(f"{label:<22}" + "".join(f"{f:>7.3f}" for f in frequencies))

print()
print("The distribution barely moves. That invariance under rescaling is the")
print("strongest argument that Benford is the only law it could have been.")

print()
# Multiplying many random factors also produces Benford behaviour, by the
# central limit theorem acting on the logarithms.
products = np.prod(rng.uniform(0.5, 5.0, size=(80_000, 12)), axis=1)
frequencies = [(leading_digit(products) == d).mean() for d in digits]
print("Products of many random factors converge to Benford too:")
print(f"  {'observed':<10}" + "".join(f"{f:>7.3f}" for f in frequencies))
print(f"  {'Benford':<10}" + "".join(f"{p:>7.3f}" for p in benford))`}
</PythonCell>

That last result is the practical engine behind the law. Quantities that arise from **multiplying**
many independent factors — compound growth, prices built from margins on costs, populations
growing at varying rates — have log values that are sums of many independent terms. The central
limit theorem spreads those sums out, and a spread-out distribution of logarithms produces a nearly
uniform mantissa, which is Benford.

<Callout title="Benford's law is a property of the generating process" variant="warning">
  <p>
    It is not a law of nature that all numbers obey. It follows from a specific kind of
    process — multiplicative, spanning several orders of magnitude, unconstrained. Data generated
    differently will not conform, and no amount of being "natural" changes that. The next lesson is
    about telling the two cases apart <em>before</em> drawing conclusions from a test.
  </p>
</Callout>

Everything in the previous lesson — the standard errors, the p-values, the intervals — is
conditional on assumptions that nothing in the fitting process checks. statsmodels will report a
p-value of $10^{-13}$ for a model whose assumptions are comprehensively violated. Checking them is
your job, and it is the part of regression most often skipped.

<h2 id="the-four">The four assumptions</h2>

Remembered as **LINE**, minus the redundant letter:

1. **Linearity.** The relationship between predictors and response really is linear (in the
   coefficients). If it is curved, the fitted line is systematically wrong in a pattern.
2. **Independence.** Observations do not influence one another. Violated by time series, by
   repeated measurements on the same subject, and by clustered sampling.
3. **Homoskedasticity.** The error variance is constant across the range of fitted values. When
   it fans out, the standard errors are wrong — usually too small.
4. **Normality of residuals.** The errors are approximately normally distributed. Needed for
   exact p-values and intervals in small samples; the central limit theorem makes this
   increasingly forgiving as $n$ grows.

Note what is **not** on the list. The predictors need not be normally distributed. They need not
be uncorrelated with each other — that is multicollinearity, an interpretability problem rather
than a validity one. And $y$ itself need not be normal; it is the *residuals* that matter.

<h2 id="residual-plots">Residual plots</h2>

Four plots diagnose almost everything. Learn to read them and you rarely need a formal test to
know something is wrong.

- **Residuals vs. fitted.** Should be a structureless horizontal band. Curvature means the
  linearity assumption failed. A funnel means heteroskedasticity.
- **Q-Q plot of residuals.** Points on the diagonal means normal. Ends curving away means heavy
  tails; a systematic bow means skew.
- **Scale-location.** The square root of standardised residuals against fitted values, with a flat
  trend if variance is constant. Often clearer than the first plot for spotting a funnel.
- **Residuals vs. leverage.** Identifies points with the influence to move the fit on their own.
  Cook's distance above about $4/n$ deserves a look.

<PythonCell label="The four diagnostic plots">
{`import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
from scipy import stats

cars = pd.read_csv("fuel-efficiency.csv")
model = smf.ols("city_kpl ~ engine_litres + weight_kg + power_kw", data=cars).fit()

influence = model.get_influence()
standardised = influence.resid_studentized_internal
leverage = influence.hat_matrix_diag
cooks = influence.cooks_distance[0]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(9.5, 6.5))

axes[0, 0].scatter(model.fittedvalues, model.resid, s=12, alpha=0.6)
axes[0, 0].axhline(0, linewidth=1)
axes[0, 0].set_xlabel("fitted")
axes[0, 0].set_ylabel("residual")
axes[0, 0].set_title("Residuals vs fitted")

stats.probplot(standardised, dist="norm", plot=axes[0, 1])
axes[0, 1].set_title("Normal Q-Q")

axes[1, 0].scatter(model.fittedvalues, np.sqrt(np.abs(standardised)), s=12, alpha=0.6)
axes[1, 0].set_xlabel("fitted")
axes[1, 0].set_ylabel("sqrt |std. residual|")
axes[1, 0].set_title("Scale-location")

axes[1, 1].scatter(leverage, standardised, s=12, alpha=0.6)
axes[1, 1].axhline(0, linewidth=1)
axes[1, 1].set_xlabel("leverage")
axes[1, 1].set_ylabel("std. residual")
axes[1, 1].set_title("Residuals vs leverage")

plt.tight_layout()

threshold = 4 / len(cars)
flagged = np.where(cooks > threshold)[0]
print(f"Cook's distance threshold 4/n = {threshold:.4f}")
print(f"{len(flagged)} influential observations flagged:")
print(cars.loc[flagged[:8], ["model_id", "engine_litres", "weight_kg", "city_kpl"]].to_string(index=False))`}
</PythonCell>

The Q-Q plot is the striking one. The upper tail departs sharply from the diagonal: a group of
observations has residuals far larger than a normal distribution would produce. Those are the
hybrids, and they also appear in the Cook's distance list.

<h2 id="formal-tests">Formal tests</h2>

Plots are better for diagnosis; tests are better for reporting. Three cover the assumptions that
can be tested directly.

- **Jarque-Bera** or **Omnibus** for normality of residuals. A small p-value rejects normality.
- **Breusch-Pagan** or **White** for homoskedasticity. A small p-value indicates the variance
  depends on the predictors.
- **Durbin-Watson** for autocorrelation. Ranges 0 to 4; near 2 means no autocorrelation, below 1.5
  suggests positive autocorrelation.

<Callout title="Tests get more sensitive as n grows" variant="warning">
  <p>
    With 100,000 observations, a normality test will reject essentially any real dataset, because
    no real data is exactly normal and the test has the power to detect it. That does not mean the
    model is unusable. Read the test alongside the plot and ask how <em>badly</em> the assumption
    fails, not merely whether it fails.
  </p>
</Callout>

<PythonCell label="All three tests">
{`from statsmodels.stats.stattools import jarque_bera, durbin_watson
from statsmodels.stats.diagnostic import het_breuschpagan

jb_stat, jb_p, skew, kurtosis = jarque_bera(model.resid)
print("NORMALITY (Jarque-Bera)")
print(f"  statistic {jb_stat:10.2f}   p {jb_p:.3e}")
print(f"  skew      {skew:10.3f}   (0 for a normal distribution)")
print(f"  kurtosis  {kurtosis:10.3f}   (3 for a normal distribution)")
print(f"  verdict:  {'residuals are NOT normal' if jb_p < 0.05 else 'consistent with normal'}")

print()
bp_stat, bp_p, f_stat, f_p = het_breuschpagan(model.resid, model.model.exog)
print("HOMOSKEDASTICITY (Breusch-Pagan)")
print(f"  statistic {bp_stat:10.2f}   p {bp_p:.4f}")
print(f"  verdict:  {'variance is NOT constant' if bp_p < 0.05 else 'consistent with constant variance'}")

print()
dw = durbin_watson(model.resid)
print("INDEPENDENCE (Durbin-Watson)")
print(f"  statistic {dw:10.3f}   (2.0 means no autocorrelation)")
print(f"  verdict:  {'no autocorrelation' if 1.5 < dw < 2.5 else 'autocorrelation present'}")`}
</PythonCell>

The verdict on this model: independence is fine, and normality fails badly — skew near 2.8 and
kurtosis near 15, against 0 and 3 for a normal distribution. That is not a marginal failure to be
waved through.

<h2 id="what-to-do">What to do when one fails</h2>

| Assumption | Symptom | Options |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linearity | Curvature in residuals vs. fitted | Add a polynomial or interaction term; transform a predictor; use a non-linear model |
| Independence | Durbin-Watson far from 2 | Model the dependence explicitly — time-series methods (chapter&nbsp;7) or mixed models |
| Homoskedasticity | Funnel shape; small Breusch-Pagan p | Transform the response (log is common); use robust (HC) standard errors; weighted least squares |
| Normality | Q-Q tails depart; small Jarque-Bera p | Often ignorable at large $n$; otherwise transform the response, or find the omitted variable causing the outliers |

The last row is the interesting one here, because the residual skew has an identifiable cause
rather than being a distributional quirk. The hybrids are a distinct group the model has no
predictor for. Give it one, and the problem largely resolves — which is the general lesson:
**non-normal residuals are frequently a symptom of a missing variable, not of a need to transform
anything.**

<PythonCell label="Fixing it by adding the missing variable">
{`# The hybrids are identifiable: far more efficient than their size predicts.
predicted = model.fittedvalues
cars = cars.assign(is_hybrid=(model.resid > 4.0).astype(int))
print(f"flagged as hybrid: {cars['is_hybrid'].sum()} of {len(cars)}")

better = smf.ols(
    "city_kpl ~ engine_litres + weight_kg + power_kw + is_hybrid", data=cars
).fit()

jb_before = jarque_bera(model.resid)
jb_after = jarque_bera(better.resid)

print()
print(f"{'':<22}{'before':>12}{'after':>12}")
print(f"{'R-squared':<22}{model.rsquared:>12.4f}{better.rsquared:>12.4f}")
print(f"{'residual sd (kpl)':<22}{np.sqrt(model.scale):>12.4f}{np.sqrt(better.scale):>12.4f}")
print(f"{'residual skew':<22}{jb_before[2]:>12.3f}{jb_after[2]:>12.3f}")
print(f"{'residual kurtosis':<22}{jb_before[3]:>12.3f}{jb_after[3]:>12.3f}")
print(f"{'Jarque-Bera p':<22}{jb_before[1]:>12.2e}{jb_after[1]:>12.2e}")

print()
print("Coefficients move toward the values the data were generated from:")
truth = {"Intercept": 24.5, "engine_litres": -1.85, "weight_kg": -0.0042, "power_kw": 0.021}
print(f"{'term':<16}{'before':>11}{'after':>11}{'truth':>11}")
for name, value in truth.items():
    print(f"{name:<16}{model.params[name]:>11.5f}{better.params[name]:>11.5f}{value:>11.5f}")`}
</PythonCell>

Adding one indicator variable lifts $R^2$ substantially, cuts the residual standard deviation, and
pulls every coefficient back toward the values the data were actually generated from. The
diagnostics were not a formality — they were pointing at a real, fixable defect in the model.

<Callout title="Flagging outliers by their residuals is circular" variant="pitfall">
  <p>
    The cell above identifies hybrids using residuals from the very model being corrected, which
    would be indefensible in real analysis: you can always improve a fit by adding an indicator for
    the points it fits worst. It is legitimate here <em>only</em> because we know from how the data
    were generated that a genuine subgroup exists. In practice, find the variable in the world —
    a powertrain column, a customer segment, a manufacturing line — rather than in the residuals.
  </p>
</Callout>

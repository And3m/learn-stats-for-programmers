<h2 id="coefficients">What a coefficient means</h2>

Fitting the model from the previous lesson gives roughly

$$
\widehat{\text{kpl}} = 23.42 - 2.45\,\text{engine} - 0.00356\,\text{weight} + 0.0326\,\text{power}
$$

The engine coefficient says: **among vehicles of the same weight and the same power output**, one
extra litre of displacement is associated with 2.45 fewer kilometres per litre.

Three qualifications are packed into that sentence, and dropping any of them turns a defensible
statement into a wrong one.

1. **"Holding the others fixed."** This is not the effect of a bigger engine in general — bigger
   engines come with more weight and more power, and those have their own effects. It is the
   effect of displacement *net of* the others.
2. **"Is associated with."** Regression measures association. Causation requires an argument
   about how the data were generated, which no amount of fitting supplies.
3. **The units are the data's units.** Per litre, per kilogram, per kilowatt. A coefficient of
   $-0.00356$ on weight looks negligible until you notice that vehicles differ by hundreds of
   kilograms: 300 kg costs about 1.07 kpl.

<Callout title="The power coefficient is positive. Is that plausible?" variant="warning">
  <p>
    On its own, "more power improves fuel economy" is nonsense. But the coefficient is not on its
    own — it is conditional on engine size and weight being held fixed. Among vehicles of identical
    displacement and mass, the more powerful one is likely to be the more modern, more efficient
    engine. Sign flips like this are normal in multiple regression and are one of the clearest
    reminders that these are conditional statements, not marginal ones. Note also that{" "}
    <code>power_kw</code> is correlated 0.94 with <code>engine_litres</code> — see below.
  </p>
</Callout>

<PythonCell label="Coefficients in units people care about">
{`import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

cars = pd.read_csv("fuel-efficiency.csv")
model = smf.ols("city_kpl ~ engine_litres + weight_kg + power_kw", data=cars).fit()

print("Per-unit effects:")
for name in ["engine_litres", "weight_kg", "power_kw"]:
    print(f"  {name:<14} {model.params[name]:+.5f} kpl per unit")

print()
print("Rescaled to differences people actually encounter:")
print(f"  +1.0 litre of displacement : {model.params['engine_litres'] * 1.0:+.2f} kpl")
print(f"  +300 kg of kerb weight     : {model.params['weight_kg'] * 300:+.2f} kpl")
print(f"  +50 kW of power            : {model.params['power_kw'] * 50:+.2f} kpl")

print()
# Standardised coefficients put predictors on a common footing.
z = cars.copy()
for column in ["engine_litres", "weight_kg", "power_kw", "city_kpl"]:
    z[column] = (z[column] - z[column].mean()) / z[column].std(ddof=1)
standardised = smf.ols("city_kpl ~ engine_litres + weight_kg + power_kw", data=z).fit()
print("Standardised (effect of a one-SD change, in SDs of the response):")
for name in ["engine_litres", "weight_kg", "power_kw"]:
    print(f"  {name:<14} {standardised.params[name]:+.4f}")`}
</PythonCell>

Standardised coefficients answer "which predictor matters most?" in a way raw coefficients cannot,
because raw coefficients depend entirely on the units each variable happens to be measured in.

<h2 id="significance">Significance, honestly</h2>

A p-value is the probability of observing a coefficient at least this far from zero **if the true
coefficient were exactly zero** and every model assumption held. That is all it is.

What it is **not**:

- Not the probability the null hypothesis is true.
- Not the probability the result is a fluke.
- Not a measure of effect size. With 260 observations a trivially small effect can be highly
  significant; with 12 observations a large one may not be.
- Not a licence to ignore the confidence interval, which carries strictly more information.

The 0.05 threshold is a convention, not a law of nature. A p-value of 0.049 and one of 0.051 are
essentially the same evidence, and treating them as categorically different is how a great deal of
poor analysis happens.

<Callout title="What multiple testing does to p-values" variant="pitfall">
  <p>
    Test twenty independent null hypotheses at the 5% level and you should expect one "significant"
    result purely by chance. Adding predictors until something reaches p &lt; 0.05 guarantees you
    find one. If you test many things, either correct for it (Bonferroni divides the threshold by
    the number of tests) or state plainly that the analysis was exploratory.
  </p>
</Callout>

<h2 id="intervals">Confidence vs. prediction intervals</h2>

These are routinely confused and answer genuinely different questions.

A **confidence interval** for the mean response at some $x$ says: where is the *average* kpl of
all vehicles with these characteristics? Its width depends only on uncertainty in the estimated
coefficients, so it shrinks toward zero as the sample grows.

A **prediction interval** for a new observation says: where will *this particular vehicle* land?
It must account for coefficient uncertainty **plus** the irreducible scatter of individual
vehicles around the line. It is always wider, and it does **not** shrink to zero — no amount of
data removes the noise term $\varepsilon$.

$$
\text{CI width} \propto \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{y})}
\qquad
\text{PI width} \propto \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{y}) + \sigma^2}
$$

{`import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

    "engine_litres": [2.0],
    "weight_kg": [1450],
    "power_kw": [120],

      f"   width {frame['mean_ci_upper'][0] - frame['mean_ci_lower'][0]:.2f}")
print(f"95% PI  (one car)   [{frame['obs_ci_lower'][0]:.2f}, {frame['obs_ci_upper'][0]:.2f}]"
      f"   width {frame['obs_ci_upper'][0] - frame['obs_ci_lower'][0]:.2f}")

<h2 id="prediction">Predicting new values, and when not to</h2>

Two rules govern honest prediction from a regression.

**Do not extrapolate.** The model has seen engines from 1.0 to 5.0 litres. Asking it about a
9-litre engine produces a number with no evidence behind it, and the confidence interval will not
warn you — the interval widens, but it is computed under the assumption that the linear
relationship continues, which is exactly the assumption at issue.

**Watch the condition number.** For this model it is around 12,900, which is very high. The
predictors are correlated at 0.85 to 0.94, so the fit cannot cleanly separate their individual
contributions. Predictions remain fine — collinearity does not bias fitted values — but the
*individual coefficients* are unstable, and that is what people usually want to interpret.

<PythonCell label="Multicollinearity, measured">
{`from statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence import variance_inflation_factor
import statsmodels.api as sm

X = sm.add_constant(cars[["engine_litres", "weight_kg", "power_kw"]])

print("Variance inflation factors (1 = no collinearity, >5 worth noting, >10 serious):")
for i, name in enumerate(X.columns):
    if name == "const":
        continue
    print(f"  {name:<14} VIF = {variance_inflation_factor(X.values, i):6.2f}")

print()
print(f"condition number: {model.condition_number:,.0f}")

print()
print("Dropping the most collinear predictor moves the others substantially:")
reduced = smf.ols("city_kpl ~ engine_litres + weight_kg", data=cars).fit()
comparison = pd.DataFrame({
    "full model": model.params,
    "without power_kw": reduced.params,
}).dropna()
print(comparison.round(5).to_string())
print()
print(f"R2 full {model.rsquared:.4f}   R2 reduced {reduced.rsquared:.4f}"
      "   <- prediction barely suffers")`}
</PythonCell>

The last comparison is the practical signature of multicollinearity: dropping a predictor barely
moves $R^2$ but substantially moves the remaining coefficients. If your conclusion depends on the
exact value of one coefficient, and that value swings when a correlated predictor is added or
removed, the conclusion is not safe.

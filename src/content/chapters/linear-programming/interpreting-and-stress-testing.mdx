The optimal plan is rarely the most valuable output of a linear program. What managers actually
want is the answer to two questions: *what should we invest in?* and *how wrong can our estimates
be before this plan stops being right?*

Both are answered from the same solve.

<h2 id="binding-constraints">Binding constraints</h2>

At the optimum, some resources are exhausted and some are not.

A **binding** constraint has zero slack. It is what stopped the objective from going higher, so
more of it is worth something. A **non-binding** constraint has slack left over — more of it is
worth exactly nothing, because you already cannot use what you have.

This one distinction is the highest-value output of the model. Every proposal to buy more capacity
can be checked against it in seconds.

<PythonCell label="Where the money is">
{`import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.optimize import linprog

products = ["standard", "deluxe", "premium"]
resources = ["machining (hrs)", "assembly (hrs)", "aluminium (kg)"]

profit = np.array([40.0, 68.0, 112.0])
usage = np.array([
    [1.2, 2.0, 3.2],
    [0.5, 1.6, 2.6],
    [3.0, 4.5, 7.0],
])
available = np.array([400.0, 300.0, 1200.0])
bounds = [(20, None), (0, None), (0, 60)]

def solve(limits):
    return linprog(c=-profit, A_ub=usage, b_ub=limits, bounds=bounds, method="highs")

base = solve(available)
print(f"optimal profit: {-base.fun:,.2f}")
print(f"plan: " + ", ".join(f"{n} {q:.2f}" for n, q in zip(products, base.x)))
print()

print(f"{'resource':<18}{'slack':>10}   {'value of +1 unit':>18}")
for index, (name, spare) in enumerate(zip(resources, base.slack)):
    bumped = available.copy()
    bumped[index] += 1
    gain = -solve(bumped).fun + base.fun
    label = "BINDING" if spare < 1e-6 else f"slack {spare:,.1f}"
    print(f"{name:<18}{label:>10}   {gain:>18,.4f}")

print()
print("Buying aluminium would be money burned - there are hundreds of")
print("kilograms already sitting unused.")`}

<h2 id="shadow-prices">Shadow prices</h2>

The number in that last column is the **shadow price** (or dual value): the change in the
objective from one additional unit of a resource. It is the most you should rationally pay for
that extra unit.

HiGHS reports these directly, so you do not need to re-solve — though re-solving is a good way to
confirm you are reading them correctly.

Two properties matter.

**Non-binding constraints have a shadow price of exactly zero.** More of an already-surplus
resource cannot help.

**Shadow prices are marginal and local.** A machining shadow price of £32.61 does not mean 100
extra hours are worth £3,261. As you add capacity the optimal plan shifts, and eventually a
*different* constraint becomes binding — at which point the shadow price drops, often to zero.
Every shadow price carries an implied validity range, and extrapolating past it is the most common
misuse of this output.

<PythonCell label="Shadow prices, and where they stop applying">
{`print("Shadow prices reported by HiGHS (sign convention: negative because we minimise):")

          f"({'binding' if spare < 1e-6 else 'slack -> zero, as expected'})")

print()
print("But the price only holds over a range. Adding machining hours:")
print(f"{'extra hours':>12}{'profit':>14}{'marginal value':>17}")

for extra in [0, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800]:
    bumped = available.copy()
    bumped[0] += extra
    value = -solve(bumped).fun
    marginal = (value - previous) / 25 if extra else float("nan")
    print(f"{extra:>12}{value:>14,.2f}"
          + (f"{marginal:>17.4f}" if extra else f"{'-':>17}"))
    previous = value

extras = np.linspace(0, 700, 60)

for extra in extras:
    bumped = available.copy()
    bumped[0] += extra
    profits.append(-solve(bumped).fun)

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(extras, profits, linewidth=2)
ax.set_xlabel("additional machining hours")
ax.set_ylabel("optimal profit")
ax.set_title("Piecewise linear: each kink is a new constraint becoming binding")

print("The curve flattens. Past a point, machining is no longer the")
print("bottleneck and further hours are worth nothing.")`}
</PythonCell>

<Callout title="A shadow price is an upper bound on what to pay" variant="warning">
  <p>
    If overtime machining costs £25 an hour and the shadow price is £32.61, the first extra hours
    are worth buying. That does not make 500 hours worth buying — check the range first. And the
    shadow price assumes everything else about the formulation is correct, so it inherits every
    error in the model's inputs.
  </p>

<h2 id="sensitivity">Sensitivity analysis</h2>

The profit coefficients and resource limits are estimates. A responsible answer says how much they
would have to be wrong before the recommendation changes.

Two complementary approaches:

**One-at-a-time.** Vary one input across a plausible range, holding the rest fixed, and watch the
plan. Simple, and it isolates which inputs matter.

**Scenario sampling.** Perturb every uncertain input simultaneously from its own distribution,
re-solve many times, and look at the distribution of outcomes. This is Monte Carlo simulation —
chapter&nbsp;9 — applied to an optimisation, and it captures interactions that one-at-a-time
analysis misses.

<PythonCell label="Two kinds of stress test">
{`print("ONE AT A TIME: how sensitive is the plan to the deluxe margin?")
print(f"{'deluxe profit':>15}{'standard':>10}{'deluxe':>9}{'premium':>9}{'total':>12}")
for margin in [50, 60, 68, 76, 85, 95]:
    adjusted = profit.copy()
    adjusted[1] = margin
    outcome = linprog(c=-adjusted, A_ub=usage, b_ub=available, bounds=bounds, method="highs")
    print(f"{margin:>15}{outcome.x[0]:>10.1f}{outcome.x[1]:>9.1f}"
          f"{outcome.x[2]:>9.1f}{-outcome.fun:>12,.0f}")

print()
print("SCENARIO SAMPLING: every margin uncertain by +/-12%, 2000 draws")
rng = np.random.default_rng(19)
draws = 2000
totals = np.empty(draws)
plans = np.empty((draws, 3))

for draw in range(draws):
    perturbed = profit * rng.uniform(0.88, 1.12, size=3)
    outcome = linprog(c=-perturbed, A_ub=usage, b_ub=available, bounds=bounds, method="highs")
    plans[draw] = outcome.x
    totals[draw] = profit @ outcome.x        # value the plan at the TRUE margins

print(f"  profit under the baseline plan      {profit @ base.x:>12,.2f}")
print(f"  mean profit of the perturbed plans  {totals.mean():>12,.2f}")
print(f"  5th percentile                      {np.percentile(totals, 5):>12,.2f}")
print(f"  worst case seen                     {totals.min():>12,.2f}")

print()
print("  how often each product appears in the plan:")
for name, column in zip(products, plans.T):
    print(f"    {name:<10} produced in {(column > 1e-6).mean():>6.1%} of scenarios, "
          f"median {np.median(column):>7.1f} units")

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.hist(totals, bins=40)
ax.axvline(profit @ base.x, linewidth=2)
ax.set_xlabel("profit at true margins")
ax.set_ylabel("scenarios")
ax.set_title("Cost of optimising against mis-estimated margins")`}
</PythonCell>

The final experiment is the honest one. Each scenario optimises against *estimated* margins, then
the resulting plan is valued at the *true* margins — which is exactly what happens in reality. The
gap between the baseline profit and the mean of that distribution is the cost of estimation error,
and it is usually much smaller than people fear. Near the optimum the objective is flat, so a
slightly wrong plan is only slightly worse.

That flatness is the reassuring conclusion of this chapter: linear programs are generally robust
to moderate input error. What they are not robust to is a **missing constraint**, which produces a
plan that is optimal for a problem you do not have.

<h2 id="shape-of-a-lesson">The shape of a lesson</h2>

Every lesson follows the same rhythm, on purpose. Once you know the shape you can skim the parts
you already have and slow down where you do not.

1. **Learning objectives.** Three or so statements of what you should be able to do afterwards.
   Worth reading twice — they are also a decent self-test on the way out.
2. **The idea, in words.** Why the technique exists and what problem it solves. No formula yet.
3. **The idea, in symbols.** The formula, with every symbol named, and where it comes from when
   the derivation is short enough to be illuminating rather than tedious.
4. **The idea, in code.** A runnable cell you can edit. The code is always short enough to read
   in one pass; where a real analysis would need forty lines of setup, the setup is generated for
   you and the interesting part is left visible.
5. **The conditions.** When the technique applies, and — more usefully — how it fails when it
   does not. This section is often the longest.
6. **A check.** Two or three questions with explanations for every option, right and wrong.

The right-hand rail lists the sections and cites the corresponding pages in the book this
syllabus follows, if you want a second treatment of the same material.

<h2 id="the-runtime">About the Python runtime</h2>

The interpreter is real CPython 3.14 compiled to WebAssembly. It is not a simulation and not a
sandboxed subset — `import this` works, and so does everything else you would expect.

A few practical notes:

- **It downloads on demand.** The interpreter is roughly 12&nbsp;MB, and each library is more on
  top: NumPy is small, SciPy and scikit-learn are not. Nothing downloads until you press
  **Start Python**, and lessons only load the libraries they actually use.
- **It is per-page.** Leaving a lesson tears the interpreter down. Coming back starts it again,
  though the browser cache makes the second start much faster.
- **`plt.show()` is optional.** Any figure left open when a cell finishes is captured and rendered
  automatically, using the colours of whichever theme you are reading in. Switch between light and
  dark and re-run a plot to see it.
- **Files are virtual.** Lessons that need data get a CSV written into an in-memory filesystem
  before the first run, so `pd.read_csv("payments.csv")` just works. Writing files is fine too;
  they vanish when you leave.

<h2 id="not-covered">What this course does not cover</h2>

Being explicit about the boundary is more useful than pretending there isn't one.

- **Deep learning.** No neural networks, no backpropagation, no transformers. Different toolkit,
  different course.
- **Causal inference.** This course will teach you to fit a regression and read its coefficients
  honestly. It will not teach you to claim those coefficients are causal — that needs
  instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, and a great deal of care about study design.
- **Bayesian modelling.** Bayes' theorem appears in chapter&nbsp;3 as a rule for updating
  probabilities, which it is. Full Bayesian inference — priors, posteriors, MCMC — does not.
- **Big data engineering.** Everything here fits comfortably in memory. Spark, Dask and the rest
  are orthogonal concerns.
- **Proofs.** Derivations appear when they build intuition. Measure-theoretic foundations do not.

What you should have at the end is the ability to pick the right technique for a decision, apply
it correctly in Python, check that its assumptions hold, and state the conclusion with the right
amount of confidence — no more and no less.

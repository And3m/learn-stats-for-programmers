Most working programmers already do statistics, whether or not they call it that. You compute a
p95 latency. You compare last week's conversion rate to this week's. You look at a dashboard and
decide that a spike is or is not worth waking someone up for. Those are all statistical acts.

What is usually missing is not the arithmetic. It is the second half: knowing what the number
entitles you to say.

<h2 id="the-gap">The gap this course closes</h2>

Consider a concrete case. Your team ships a change and conversion moves from 4.1% to 4.6%. Is
that real?

Almost everyone can compute the difference: half a percentage point, a 12% relative lift. Rather
fewer can answer the questions that decide whether to act on it. How many visitors were in each
group? How much does this metric bounce around week to week even when nothing changes? If you had
run the same test against no change at all, how often would you have seen a gap this large by
luck alone?

Those questions have precise answers, and the answers sometimes reverse the decision entirely. A
half-point lift on 400 visitors is noise. The same lift on 400,000 is a result you can build a
quarter's roadmap around. The arithmetic is identical; the conclusion is not.

<Callout title="The recurring pattern">
  Nearly every technique in this course exists to answer one of two questions:{" "}
  <strong>how much of what I am seeing is signal, and how much is noise?</strong> and{" "}
  <strong>given what I have seen, what should I now expect?</strong> Regression, hypothesis
  tests, control charts, Monte Carlo simulation and Markov chains are all different instruments
  pointed at those same two questions.
</Callout>

<h2 id="stats-vs-quant">Statistics vs. quantitative methods</h2>

Three labels get used loosely, and it helps to keep them apart.

**Descriptive statistics** summarise data you already have. A mean, a median, a standard
deviation, a histogram. They make no claim beyond the rows in front of you. If you have logged
every request your service handled last month, the mean latency of those requests is simply a
fact — there is nothing to infer.

**Inferential statistics** use a sample to make claims about a population you did not fully
observe. This is where uncertainty enters, and where most of the interesting machinery lives:
confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, regression coefficients. The moment you say "and next
month should look similar," you have left description behind.

**Quantitative methods** is the broader family: anything that turns a decision problem into a
model you can compute with. Linear programming, decision analysis, project scheduling and
simulation are all quantitative methods that are barely statistical at all — they are about
optimisation and structured choice under constraint. Chapters 8, 10 and 13 of this course are
firmly in that territory.

| | Answers | Uncertainty | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Descriptive | What happened? | None — it is a fact about your data | p95 latency last month |
| Inferential | What is probably true in general? | Central | Is the new version faster? |
| Quantitative methods | What should we do? | Sometimes | Which jobs to run on which machines |

The distinction matters because the failure modes differ. Descriptive statistics fail when you
summarise badly — a mean over a bimodal distribution, a percentage without its denominator.
Inferential statistics fail when the assumptions behind the inference do not hold. Quantitative
methods fail when the model omits the constraint that actually binds.

<h2 id="where-it-shows-up">Where it shows up in real work</h2>

Some of these will be familiar; the point is that each has a named technique behind it, and this
course covers all of them.

- **Is this alert real?** A metric crossed a threshold. Control charts (chapter&nbsp;14) exist
  precisely to separate the variation a process always has from the variation that means
  something changed.
- **Which features drive the outcome?** Regression (chapters&nbsp;4 and&nbsp;5) and tree
  ensembles (chapter&nbsp;6) both answer this, and they disagree in instructive ways.
- **How long will this project take?** PERT and critical-path analysis (chapter&nbsp;13) turn
  three-point estimates into a probability of hitting a date, rather than a single optimistic
  number.
- **What will next quarter look like?** Time-series models (chapter&nbsp;7) separate trend from
  seasonality from noise and forecast with an interval, not a point.
- **These invoices look odd.** Benford's law (chapter&nbsp;12) is a genuinely useful first-pass
  anomaly detector over financial data.
- **We cannot do everything — what should we do?** Linear programming (chapter&nbsp;8) finds the
  best feasible plan given resource limits, instead of the first plan that fits.

<Callout title="A warning worth taking seriously" variant="warning">
  Every one of these techniques will produce a confident-looking number from data that violates
  its assumptions. Nothing stops you fitting a linear regression to a curve, or an ARIMA model
  to a non-stationary series, or reading significance into a test you ran twenty times. Knowing
  when a method applies is the harder and more valuable half of the skill, which is why nearly
  every chapter here spends as long on the conditions as on the calculation.
</Callout>

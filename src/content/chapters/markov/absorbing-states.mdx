<h2 id="what-absorbs">What makes a state absorbing</h2>

A state $i$ is **absorbing** if $P_{ii} = 1$: once entered, it is never left. A chain with at least
one absorbing state, reachable from every transient state, is an **absorbing chain**.

These chains behave completely differently from the previous lessons'. There is no interesting
steady state — eventually everything ends up absorbed, so the limiting distribution puts all its
mass on the absorbing states. The interesting questions are different:

- **How long** until absorption?
- **Which** absorbing state, when there is more than one?
- **How much time** is spent in each transient state along the way?

The scenario: a customer lifecycle. Trial, active and at-risk are transient; churned and upgraded
are absorbing.

<h2 id="fundamental-matrix">The fundamental matrix</h2>

Reorder the states so transient ones come first, giving the **canonical form**:

$$
P = \begin{bmatrix} Q & R \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}
$$

where $Q$ holds transient-to-transient probabilities, $R$ transient-to-absorbing, and the bottom
row expresses that absorbing states never leave.

The **fundamental matrix** is

$$
N = (I - Q)^{-1}
$$

and its entries have a precise meaning: $N_{ij}$ is the **expected number of visits to transient
state $j$, starting from transient state $i$**, before absorption.

Why the inverse? Because $N = I + Q + Q^2 + Q^3 + \cdots$ — the expected visit count is the sum
over all step counts of the probability of being there at that step. That geometric series sums to
$(I - Q)^{-1}$ exactly when $Q$'s spectral radius is below one, which is guaranteed when every
transient state can reach absorption.

<h2 id="expected-steps">Expected steps and final outcomes</h2>

Two results follow immediately from $N$.

**Expected steps to absorption** is the row sum of $N$:

$$
t = N\mathbf{1}
$$

Total time before absorption is the sum of time spent in each transient state, which is exactly
what a row of $N$ contains.

**Absorption probabilities** are

$$
B = NR
$$

$B_{ij}$ is the probability of eventually being absorbed into state $j$, starting from transient
state $i$. The reasoning: spend $N_{ik}$ expected visits in each transient state $k$, and from
each of those visits absorb into $j$ with probability $R_{kj}$.

<PythonCell label="What would an intervention be worth?">
{`import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def outcomes_for(at_risk_recovery):
    """Recompute the model if at-risk customers recover more often."""
    modified = P.copy()
    row = states.index("at risk")
    churn_index, active_index = states.index("churned"), states.index("active")
    # Move probability mass from churn back into active.
    shift = at_risk_recovery - P[row, active_index]
    modified[row, active_index] += shift
    modified[row, churn_index] -= shift

    q = modified[np.ix_(transient_index, transient_index)]
    r = modified[np.ix_(transient_index, absorbing_index)]
    n = np.linalg.inv(np.eye(len(transient)) - q)
    return (n @ r)[0, 1], n.sum(axis=1)[0]      # P(upgrade | trial), months

recoveries = np.linspace(0.25, 0.55, 40)
upgrade_rates, lifetimes = zip(*[outcomes_for(value) for value in recoveries])

print(f"{'at-risk recovery':>18}{'P(upgrade)':>13}{'months':>10}")
for value in [0.25, 0.30, 0.35, 0.45, 0.55]:
    upgrade, months = outcomes_for(value)
    print(f"{value:>18.2f}{upgrade:>13.4f}{months:>10.2f}")

fig, (left, right) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.5, 3.4))
left.plot(recoveries, upgrade_rates, linewidth=2)
left.axvline(P[states.index("at risk"), states.index("active")], linewidth=1, linestyle="--")
left.set_xlabel("P(at risk -> active)")
left.set_ylabel("P(eventually upgrade)")
left.set_title("Value of a save campaign")

right.plot(recoveries, lifetimes, linewidth=2)
right.set_xlabel("P(at risk -> active)")
right.set_ylabel("expected months")
right.set_title("Effect on customer lifetime")
plt.tight_layout()

baseline_upgrade, baseline_months = outcomes_for(0.25)
improved_upgrade, improved_months = outcomes_for(0.40)
print()
print("Raising at-risk recovery from 25% to 40% would:")
print(f"  lift the eventual upgrade rate from {baseline_upgrade:.1%} to {improved_upgrade:.1%}")
print(f"  extend expected lifetime from {baseline_months:.1f} to {improved_months:.1f} months")
print()
print("That is a concrete, defensible number to weigh a retention")
print("programme against - which is what the fundamental matrix buys you.")`}

<Callout title="The model assumes constant transition rates" variant="warning">
  Everything above rests on the transition probabilities staying fixed. In practice churn rates
  move with the season, the product and the competition, and the Markov property is at its
  weakest exactly where it matters most — an at-risk customer who has recovered twice before is
  genuinely different from one newly at risk. Treat these figures as a well-structured baseline,
  not a forecast.
</Callout>

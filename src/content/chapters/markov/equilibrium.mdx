<h2 id="steady-state">The steady state</h2>

The distribution the chain converges to is the **stationary** or **steady-state** distribution
$\pi$: the one that, once reached, does not change.

$$
\pi P = \pi, \qquad \sum_i \pi_i = 1, \qquad \pi_i \ge 0
$$

The first equation says advancing a step leaves it unchanged. The second and third make it a
probability distribution — and without the normalisation the equation is satisfied by any scalar
multiple, including the useless zero vector.

The practical reading: individual customers keep switching every month, but the *proportions* stop
moving because the flows into each state exactly balance the flows out. Equilibrium here is
dynamic, not static.

<h2 id="solving-it">Solving for it directly</h2>

Iterating $P$ a few hundred times works, but it is slow, approximate, and fails outright for
periodic chains. Solving the linear system is exact.

Rearranging $\pi P = \pi$ gives $\pi(P - I) = 0$, a homogeneous system. It is singular by
construction, so you replace one equation with the normalisation constraint:

$$
\pi (P - I) = 0 \quad\longrightarrow\quad
\begin{bmatrix} (P - I)^{\mathsf T} \\ \mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T} \end{bmatrix} \pi^{\mathsf T}
= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

and solve the resulting least-squares problem.

<PythonCell label="Three routes to the same answer">
{`import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

states = ["Brand A", "Brand B", "Brand C"]
P = np.array([
    [0.85, 0.10, 0.05],
    [0.08, 0.82, 0.10],
    [0.06, 0.12, 0.82],
])

n = len(states)

# 1. Iterate until it stops moving.
pi_iterative = np.full(n, 1 / n)
for step in range(10_000):
    updated = pi_iterative @ P
    if np.abs(updated - pi_iterative).sum() < 1e-14:
        break
    pi_iterative = updated
print(f"iteration      converged after {step} steps: {np.round(pi_iterative, 8)}")

# 2. Solve the linear system, replacing one equation with the normalisation.
A = np.vstack([(P - np.eye(n)).T, np.ones(n)])
b = np.append(np.zeros(n), 1.0)
pi_solved, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)
print(f"linear solve   {np.round(pi_solved, 8)}")

# 3. A high power of P - every row is the answer.
pi_power = np.linalg.matrix_power(P, 500)[0]
print(f"matrix power   {np.round(pi_power, 8)}")

print()
print(f"all three agree: {np.allclose(pi_iterative, pi_solved) and np.allclose(pi_solved, pi_power)}")

print()
print("Long-run market shares:")
for state, share in zip(states, pi_solved):
    print(f"  {state:<10}{share:>9.4f}   {'#' * int(share * 90)}")

print()
print("Verification - advancing the steady state leaves it unchanged:")
print(f"  pi     {np.round(pi_solved, 10)}")
print(f"  pi @ P {np.round(pi_solved @ P, 10)}")
print(f"  maximum difference: {np.abs(pi_solved @ P - pi_solved).max():.2e}")`}
</PythonCell>

<h2 id="eigenvector-view">The eigenvector view</h2>

Written as $\pi P = \pi$, the steady state is a **left eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue 1** —
equivalently, an eigenvector of $P^{\mathsf T}$ with eigenvalue 1.

Every stochastic matrix has 1 as an eigenvalue, because its rows sum to one. The
Perron-Frobenius theorem guarantees that for an irreducible chain the corresponding eigenvector is
unique up to scale and can be chosen non-negative — which is exactly what makes the steady state
well defined.

The *second-largest* eigenvalue modulus then controls how fast the chain converges. Convergence is
geometric at rate $|\lambda_2|$: closer to zero means fast mixing, closer to one means slow.

<PythonCell label="Eigenvalues, and the speed of convergence">
{`eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eig(P.T)

order = np.argsort(-np.abs(eigenvalues))
eigenvalues = eigenvalues[order]
eigenvectors = eigenvectors[:, order]

print("Eigenvalues of P^T, by decreasing magnitude:")
for index, value in enumerate(eigenvalues):
    print(f"  lambda_{index + 1} = {value.real:>9.6f}   |lambda| = {abs(value):.6f}")

# The eigenvector for eigenvalue 1, normalised to sum to 1.
principal = np.real(eigenvectors[:, 0])
principal = principal / principal.sum()
print()
print(f"principal eigenvector, normalised: {np.round(principal, 8)}")
print(f"matches the linear solve: {np.allclose(principal, pi_solved)}")

second = abs(eigenvalues[1])
print()
print(f"second-largest |lambda| = {second:.6f}")
print(f"distance from equilibrium falls by about {1 - second:.1%} per month")
print(f"half-life of the deviation: {np.log(0.5) / np.log(second):.1f} months")

print()
print("Observed decay compared with the eigenvalue prediction:")
current = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
previous_gap = np.abs(current - pi_solved).sum()
print(f"{'month':>7}{'distance':>12}{'ratio':>10}")
for month in range(1, 9):
    current = current @ P
    gap = np.abs(current - pi_solved).sum()
    print(f"{month:>7}{gap:>12.6f}{gap / previous_gap:>10.4f}")
    previous_gap = gap
print(f"{'':>7}{'':>12}{second:>10.4f}   <- |lambda_2|")`}
</PythonCell>

The observed ratio settles onto the second eigenvalue, exactly as the theory predicts. That gives
you a principled answer to "how long until this stabilises?" rather than iterating until the
numbers stop changing.

<Callout title="When there is no unique steady state" variant="pitfall">
  A <strong>reducible</strong> chain — one with two groups of states that cannot reach each other
  — has multiple stationary distributions, and which one you converge to depends on where you
  started. A <strong>periodic</strong> chain has a stationary distribution that it never actually
  settles into. Check irreducibility and aperiodicity before quoting a steady state as the
  long-run answer.
</Callout>

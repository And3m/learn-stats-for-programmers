<h2 id="matrix-powers">Matrix powers</h2>

One step is $\pi_1 = \pi_0 P$. Two steps is $\pi_2 = \pi_1 P = \pi_0 P^2$. In general:

$$
\pi_n = \pi_0 P^{\,n}
$$

The matrix $P^n$ has a direct interpretation of its own: $(P^n)_{ij}$ is the probability of being
in state $j$ after exactly $n$ steps, having started in state $i$. It is not merely a computational
device — it is the $n$-step transition matrix, and it answers questions the one-step matrix cannot.

This follows from the Chapman-Kolmogorov identity, which is the law of total probability applied
across intermediate states:

$$
(P^{m+n})_{ij} = \sum_k (P^m)_{ik}\,(P^n)_{kj}
$$

To get from $i$ to $j$ in $m+n$ steps you must be *somewhere* after $m$ steps; sum over the
possibilities and you have matrix multiplication.

<PythonCell label="n-step transition matrices">
{`import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

states = ["Brand A", "Brand B", "Brand C"]
P = np.array([
    [0.85, 0.10, 0.05],
    [0.08, 0.82, 0.10],
    [0.06, 0.12, 0.82],
])

for n in [1, 3, 12, 60]:
    Pn = np.linalg.matrix_power(P, n)
    print(f"P^{n}  (probability of being in each state after {n} months)")
    print(pd.DataFrame(Pn, index=states, columns=states).round(4).to_string())
    print()

print("Read a row: 'starting from Brand A, where am I after n months?'")
print("Read a column: 'who ends up in Brand B, and where did they start?'")

print()
P12 = np.linalg.matrix_power(P, 12)
print(f"A customer with Brand A today has a {P12[0, 0]:.1%} chance of still")
print(f"being with Brand A in a year, and a {P12[0, 2]:.1%} chance of having")
print("moved to Brand C.")

print()
print("Chapman-Kolmogorov, verified:")
print(f"  P^7 computed directly equals P^3 @ P^4: "
      f"{np.allclose(np.linalg.matrix_power(P, 7), np.linalg.matrix_power(P, 3) @ np.linalg.matrix_power(P, 4))}")`}
</PythonCell>

<h2 id="n-steps">n steps ahead</h2>

Projecting the market forward is now a single operation rather than a loop. It is also worth
noting how quickly the projection stops changing.

<PythonCell label="Projecting the market forward">
{`import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

pi_0 = np.array([0.40, 0.35, 0.25])

horizon = 48
trajectory = np.empty((horizon + 1, len(states)))
trajectory[0] = pi_0
for month in range(1, horizon + 1):
    trajectory[month] = trajectory[month - 1] @ P

print(f"{'month':>7}" + "".join(f"{s:>11}" for s in states) + f"{'change':>10}")
for month in [0, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48]:
    change = (np.abs(trajectory[month] - trajectory[month - 1]).sum()
              if month > 0 else float("nan"))
    row = "".join(f"{v:>11.5f}" for v in trajectory[month])
    print(f"{month:>7}{row}" + (f"{change:>10.6f}" if month > 0 else f"{'-':>10}"))

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
for index, state in enumerate(states):
    ax.plot(trajectory[:, index], linewidth=2, label=state)
ax.set_xlabel("months")
ax.set_ylabel("market share")
ax.set_title("Shares converge, and stop moving")
ax.legend(fontsize=8)

print()
print("The month-on-month change shrinks geometrically. By month 48 the")
print("shares are stable to five decimal places.")`}
</PythonCell>

<h2 id="convergence-hint">Something is converging</h2>

Two things happen as $n$ grows, and the second is the more striking.

**The state vector settles.** Successive projections differ by less and less.

**Every row of $P^n$ becomes identical.** That is the remarkable part: after enough steps, the
probability of being in each state no longer depends on where you started. The chain forgets its
initial condition entirely.

<PythonCell label="The chain forgets where it started">
{`print("How different are the rows of P^n from one another?")
print(f"{'n':>5}{'max row spread':>18}")
for n in [1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200]:
    Pn = np.linalg.matrix_power(P, n)
    spread = (Pn.max(axis=0) - Pn.min(axis=0)).max()
    print(f"{n:>5}{spread:>18.10f}")

print()
Pn = np.linalg.matrix_power(P, 200)
print("P^200 - every row is the same vector:")
print(pd.DataFrame(Pn, index=states, columns=states).round(6).to_string())

print()
print("Three very different starting points, all converging to the same place:")
starts = {
    "everyone with A": np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0]),
    "everyone with C": np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0]),
    "evenly split":    np.array([1 / 3, 1 / 3, 1 / 3]),
}
for label, start in starts.items():
    after = start @ np.linalg.matrix_power(P, 200)
    print(f"  {label:<18}" + "  ".join(f"{v:.6f}" for v in after))

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
for label, start in starts.items():
    path = [start]
    for _ in range(40):
        path.append(path[-1] @ P)
    ax.plot([row[0] for row in path], linewidth=2, label=label)
ax.set_xlabel("months")
ax.set_ylabel("Brand A share")
ax.set_title("Different starts, one destination")
ax.legend(fontsize=8)`}
</PythonCell>

<Callout title="Convergence is not automatic" variant="warning">
  This chain converges because it is <strong>irreducible</strong> (every state reachable from
  every other) and <strong>aperiodic</strong> (it does not cycle with a fixed period). A chain
  that strictly alternates between two states — <code>[[0,1],[1,0]]</code> — never settles; it
  oscillates forever. And a chain with absorbing states converges to something that <em>does</em>{" "}
  depend on where you started, which is lesson&nbsp;11.4.
</Callout>

<PythonCell label="A chain that does not converge">
{`periodic = np.array([
    [0.0, 1.0],
    [1.0, 0.0],
])

print("A strictly alternating chain:")
start = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
current = start.copy()
for step in range(6):
    print(f"  step {step}: {np.round(current, 4)}")
    current = current @ periodic

print()
print("P^n alternates between the identity and the swap - forever:")
for n in [10, 11, 100, 101]:
    print(f"  P^{n:<4} =\\n{np.linalg.matrix_power(periodic, n)}")

print()
print("This chain is irreducible but PERIODIC with period 2, so it has no")
print("limiting distribution - though it does still have a stationary one,")
print("which the next lesson computes.")`}
</PythonCell>
